# White Papers

White Papers are the most formal editorial publications in the Monad Articles domain.

They are appropriate when an idea has matured beyond ordinary commentary or exploratory research and deserves a sustained, structured technical treatment intended to remain useful as a durable public reference.

## What belongs here

A White Paper may present:

- a technical thesis,
- a conceptual model,
- a system architecture argument,
- a protocol or interface proposal,
- a governance model,
- a product or platform thesis,
- a synthesis of substantial research,
- or another formal position that benefits from explicit scope, assumptions, evidence, tradeoffs, and implications.

## Higher burden of structure

A White Paper should generally make it possible for a critical reader to answer:

- What problem is being addressed?
- What is in scope and out of scope?
- What assumptions does the argument depend on?
- What terminology is being used precisely?
- What prior art or alternatives matter?
- What exactly is being proposed or claimed?
- What evidence supports it?
- What are the strongest alternatives?
- What tradeoffs are accepted?
- What limitations remain?
- What would falsify or weaken the thesis?
- What implications follow if the argument is correct?

A polished appearance is not enough. Formality should come from clarity and inspectability rather than tone alone.

## White Papers are not specifications

A White Paper can argue that Monad should work a particular way.

That does not make the argument an implementation contract.

If the project accepts the proposal, the enforceable consequences belong in the formal engineering record:

```text
White Paper
    ↓
proposal / thesis
    ↓
review + decision
    ↓
requirements / ADRs / specifications
    ↓
implementation + verification
    ↓
System documentation
```

This separation lets a White Paper remain readable as an argument while the engineering artifacts remain precise and governable.

## White Papers and Research

Research and White Papers overlap, but their center of gravity differs.

Research asks:

> What does the investigation allow us to conclude?

A White Paper more often asks:

> Given the available evidence and assumptions, what coherent technical position, model, or proposal should readers evaluate?

Research may end without a recommendation.

A White Paper usually has a thesis.

## Revision discipline

Because White Papers are intended to be durable references, material revisions should be especially explicit.

Keep the original `published` date stable.

Use:

```yaml
status: revised
updated: YYYY-MM-DD
```

for material changes.

When a revision materially changes the thesis, model, assumptions, or conclusion, the body should explain what changed so readers can interpret older references to the paper.

If a paper is no longer recommended but remains historically meaningful, archive it rather than silently replacing the historical record.

## Recommended structure

A normal White Paper should consider:

1. Abstract
2. Problem statement
3. Scope and non-scope
4. Terminology
5. Assumptions
6. Background and prior art
7. Thesis or proposal
8. Model, architecture, or mechanism
9. Evidence and supporting argument
10. Alternatives
11. Tradeoffs
12. Limitations and falsification conditions
13. Implications
14. Conclusion
15. References and related project records

The form should be rigorous without becoming ceremonial.
