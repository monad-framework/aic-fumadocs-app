# ADR-0004 — Authority and Source-of-Truth Model

**Status:** Accepted
**Decision type:** Foundational architecture
**Requirements baseline:** RB-001
**Traceability artifact:** RTM-001
**Depends on:** ADR-0001, ADR-0002, ADR-0003
**Primary requirement families:** `REQ-AUTH`, `REQ-GOV`, `REQ-REC`, `REQ-INT`, `REQ-PROV`
**Consequential consumers:** `REQ-EXEC`, `REQ-EVD`, `REQ-VFY`, `REQ-AI`, `REQ-REL`

---

# Decision

Monad will model authority explicitly, narrowly, and compositionally.

Authority will not be inferred merely from:

* where data currently resides,
* which system most recently changed,
* which actor possesses credentials,
* which Representation is easiest to access,
* which provider is considered "primary",
* or which value appears newest.

The authoritative relationship is instead resolved from explicit scope.

Conceptually:

```text id="5p0l7a"
Authority
   │
   ├── applies to → Subject or Subject class
   ├── applies to → property / operation / lifecycle transition
   ├── exercised by → actor / role / system
   ├── represented through → Representation or native system
   ├── constrained by → policy
   └── bounded by → context / environment / time / revision
```

Monad therefore rejects a universal, undifferentiated notion of:

```text id="cbzo9j"
source of truth
```

in favor of explicit, scoped authority.

<Callout type="info" title="Core rule">
A system may contain the current value of a fact without having the authority to define that fact.

Likewise, an actor may have technical permission to perform an operation without having the organizational authority to perform it. 
</Callout>

---

# Context

Engineering systems routinely distribute state across many places.

Examples:

```text id="3cf3zu"
Requirements wording        → MDX
Issue lifecycle             → GitHub
Build status                → CI provider
Package bytes               → registry
Deployment state            → cloud platform
Policy decisions            → governance artifacts
Semantic identity           → Monad
```

It is tempting to describe one of those systems as:

```text id="ffr56l"
the source of truth
```

That phrasing becomes dangerous when the system grows.

A single Subject may have properties whose authoritative values come from different sources.

For example:

```text id="7ku6q1"
Requirement REQ-042
├── wording             → requirements repository
├── semantic identity   → Monad
├── implementation      → source repository
├── work status         → issue tracker
├── verification result → verification record
└── release inclusion   → release governance
```

No single system necessarily owns all of them.

---

# Problem

Monad must determine:

* who or what may establish a governed fact,
* which system may define a property,
* which actor may change it,
* when authority begins and ends,
* whether authority is exclusive or shared,
* whether authority is delegated,
* whether an operation is merely technically possible or actually legitimate,
* what happens when two authoritative sources disagree,
* how authority changes during migrations,
* how native-system permissions interact with Monad policy,
* and how reconciliations avoid blindly copying values in the wrong direction.

Without an explicit authority model, synchronization and automation can silently overwrite legitimate state.

---

# Decision drivers

The authority model must support:

* hybrid persistence,
* native-system integration,
* semantic identity,
* property-level ownership,
* execution governance,
* AI-mediated actions,
* reconciliation,
* policy enforcement,
* delegated authority,
* environment separation,
* auditability,
* least privilege,
* migration,
* emergency intervention,
* and future multi-organization operation.

---

# Authority is first-class

Authority MUST be modeled explicitly when consequential behavior depends on it.

It must not exist only as comments, conventions, or tribal knowledge.

---

# Authority is scoped

Authority applies to a defined scope.

Possible scope dimensions include:

* Subject,
* Subject type,
* property,
* relation,
* operation,
* lifecycle transition,
* environment,
* repository,
* account,
* organization,
* time interval,
* revision,
* or policy domain.

---

# Property-level authority

Monad adopts property-level authority as a foundational pattern.

Example:

```yaml id="duyngh"
subject: req:0191...

authority:
  title:
    source: representation:req-mdx

  wording:
    source: representation:req-mdx

  workflow_state:
    source: integration:github-project

  semantic_identity:
    source: monad

  verification_status:
    source: verification-system
```

This avoids pretending that one Representation owns every property.

---

# Whole-Subject authority

Whole-Subject authority MAY be used where appropriate.

Example:

```text id="04rr0j"
immutable package publication
```

may have most consequential properties defined by its registry record.

But the model must still permit exceptions where needed.

---

# Authority versus source

A **source** is where information is obtained.

An **authority** is what is entitled to establish or change the governed meaning.

Therefore:

```text id="o93kpm"
source ≠ authority
```

---

# Authority versus storage

Persistence location does not establish authority.

A local cache may contain the latest observed value.

It does not become authoritative because it is locally available.

---

# Authority versus observation

Observation tells Monad what appears to be true in a system.

Authority tells Monad whether that system is entitled to define the relevant state.

---

# Authority versus truth

Authority is a governance concept.

It does not guarantee factual correctness.

An authoritative Requirement may still contain a mistake.

Therefore:

```text id="fdrjuo"
authoritative ≠ correct
```

---

# Authority versus Evidence

Evidence supports or challenges Claims.

Evidence does not automatically have authority to change governed state.

---

# Authority versus Verification

Verification determines whether an expectation is satisfied according to a defined method.

Verification does not itself grant authority to mutate the system being evaluated.

---

# Authority versus approval

Approval is an act performed under authority.

It is not synonymous with authority itself.

An actor may possess approval authority without exercising it.

---

# Authority versus credentials

Credentials enable technical access.

They do not define legitimate authority.

```text id="he4wko"
credential possession
≠
governance authority
```

---

# Authority versus native permission

A provider may permit an actor to perform an action.

Monad policy may still forbid it.

Conversely, Monad may recognize an actor as authorized while the native provider denies technical permission.

These states must remain distinct.

---

# Technical capability

A capability answers:

```text id="ozuk4z"
Can this integration perform this class of operation?
```

Authority answers:

```text id="e5a5tq"
May this actor legitimately cause this operation here?
```

The operation is permissible only when all required conditions hold.

---

# Effective permission

Consequential execution SHOULD be understood as an intersection:

```text id="lnr4rn"
effective permission
=
capability
∩ native permission
∩ Monad authority
∩ applicable policy
∩ required approval
```

---

# Actor authority

Actors may include:

* humans,
* teams,
* services,
* automation,
* AI agents,
* integration principals,
* governance bodies.

Authority MUST identify the actor or actor class to which it applies where actor identity matters.

---

# Human authority

Human authority may derive from:

* role,
* ownership,
* explicit delegation,
* project membership,
* governance decision,
* organizational policy.

---

# Machine authority

Automated services MAY have delegated authority.

Machine authority should generally be narrower than the raw capabilities of its credentials.

---

# AI authority

AI systems do not acquire authority merely because they can invoke tools.

```text id="60bfcy"
tool access ≠ authority
```

AI actions must remain subject to the same governance model as other actors.

---

# AI recommendation

An AI recommendation is a proposal.

It does not become an authoritative change until the applicable policy says it may.

---

# Delegated authority

Authority MAY be delegated.

Delegation must identify:

* delegator,
* delegate,
* scope,
* allowed operations,
* constraints,
* start time,
* end time where applicable,
* revocation mechanism.

---

# Delegation cannot exceed source authority

An actor MUST NOT legitimately delegate more authority than it possesses.

Conceptually:

```text id="473ta1"
delegated_scope
⊆
delegator_scope
```

---

# Delegation provenance

Delegation SHOULD retain provenance identifying who created it and under which governing rule.

---

# Revocation

Delegated authority MUST be revocable where the underlying authority model permits revocation.

Revocation must not erase historical evidence that the authority previously existed.

---

# Time-bounded authority

Authority MAY be valid only during a specified interval.

Example:

```yaml id="tqvsfh"
authority:
  actor: service:release-bot
  operation: deployment.promote
  valid_from: 2026-08-29T00:00:00Z
  valid_until: 2026-08-30T00:00:00Z
```

---

# Revision-bounded authority

An approval or authority grant MAY be bound to an exact revision.

Example:

```text id="llqdjr"
approve release of commit abc123
```

does not imply:

```text id="i89d4n"
approve arbitrary later commits
```

---

# Environment-bounded authority

Authority MUST support environment scope.

Example:

```text id="o3dshz"
actor may deploy to staging
```

must not imply:

```text id="e8r3dn"
actor may deploy to production
```

---

# Resource-bounded authority

Authority may be scoped to a:

* repository,
* package,
* service,
* project,
* environment,
* namespace,
* account,
* cluster,
* provider resource.

---

# Operation-bounded authority

Authority MAY apply to one operation and not another.

Example:

```text id="n5o9hd"
issue.read
issue.comment
```

may be allowed while:

```text id="qrqo3e"
issue.close
```

is not.

---

# Lifecycle-transition authority

For governed Subjects, authority may be attached to specific transitions.

Example:

```text id="7wyu50"
Requirement:
draft → proposed
proposed → approved
approved → superseded
```

Different transitions may require different authority.

---

# Authority rule

An authority rule conceptually contains:

```yaml id="lo71qz"
id: auth:0191...

scope:
  subject_type: requirement
  property: lifecycle_state

actor:
  role: requirements-owner

operations:
  - transition.approve

constraints:
  from_state: proposed
  to_state: approved
```

---

# Explicit deny

The authority model SHOULD support explicit denial.

A deny may override a general allow according to policy-defined precedence.

---

# Default deny

Consequential operations SHOULD generally follow a default-deny posture when authority cannot be resolved.

Unknown authority must not become permission.

```text id="0n85w8"
unknown ≠ allowed
```

---

# Authority resolution

When deciding whether an action is authorized, Monad SHOULD evaluate:

1. the target Subject or native resource,
2. requested operation,
3. acting principal,
4. environment,
5. applicable policy,
6. delegated authority,
7. explicit denies,
8. required approval,
9. revision or state preconditions.

---

# Resolution result

Authority resolution SHOULD produce an inspectable result.

Example:

```yaml id="q8zcd8"
decision:
  result: allow

actor: actor:0191...
operation: deployment.promote
target: service:api

basis:
  - policy: POL-004
  - delegation: AUTH-019
  - approval: APR-081

evaluated_at: 2026-08-29T14:21:00Z
```

---

# Denial explanation

Denied operations SHOULD explain which rule prevented authorization where disclosure is safe.

Example:

```text id="ztms64"
Denied:
actor has staging deployment authority,
but requested environment is production.
```

---

# Unknown authority

Monad MUST represent unresolved authority explicitly.

```text id="nbwq7x"
allow
deny
unknown
```

may be preferable to forcing all authority resolution into a Boolean where the evidence is incomplete.

---

# Authority conflict

An authority conflict exists when multiple applicable authority rules cannot be simultaneously satisfied.

This is different from ordinary data divergence.

---

# Conflicting authoritative sources

Two sources may both claim authority over the same property.

Example:

```text id="w6byex"
requirements.mdx:
status = approved

issue tracker:
status = draft
```

If both are declared authoritative for the same property, Monad must surface an authority conflict rather than choosing whichever was updated last.

---

# Newest does not win automatically

Time does not establish authority.

```text id="y65o0e"
newer ≠ more authoritative
```

---

# Native does not win automatically

A native provider does not automatically own every state merely because the value exists there.

---

# Monad does not win automatically

The semantic graph does not automatically override native systems.

---

# Human does not win automatically

A human edit does not automatically outrank governed automation.

---

# Automation does not win automatically

Automation does not gain higher precedence because it is systematic or reproducible.

---

# Authority precedence

Where multiple applicable rules can coexist, precedence MUST be explicit.

Possible dimensions include:

* specificity,
* explicit deny,
* governance level,
* delegation chain,
* environment,
* property scope,
* emergency override.

Precedence must not emerge from implementation order accidentally.

---

# Specificity

A more specific rule MAY override a more general rule where policy defines such behavior.

Example:

```text id="vpj4zs"
general:
team may deploy services to staging

specific:
service-X deployments require owner approval
```

---

# Constitutional authority

Monad MAY distinguish authority levels.

Conceptually:

```text id="pudou3"
Constitution
   ↓ constrains
Policy
   ↓ constrains
Project governance
   ↓ constrains
Delegated operation authority
```

Lower layers cannot legitimately contradict higher layers.

---

# Constitutional change

Changing the rules that define authority itself should require a higher-order governance procedure.

The system must not casually rewrite its own authority hierarchy.

---

# Policy authority

Policies define generalized constraints.

Policies may themselves require authorized actors or governance processes to create or change them.

---

# Decision authority

ADRs and other formal decisions may establish architectural authority where the project governance model recognizes them.

An ADR is not authoritative merely because a file has the prefix `ADR-`.

Its authority derives from the governance process that recognizes it.

---

# Requirements authority

A Requirement may be authoritative because it has reached an approved governed state.

Draft text does not become binding merely because it exists.

---

# State authority

The authoritative source for workflow state MAY differ from the authoritative source for semantic content.

This is expected and supported.

---

# Representation-scoped authority

Authority may apply to a specific Representation.

Example:

```text id="8hplmk"
MDX file
authoritative for authored wording
```

---

# Native-system authority

A native system MAY be authoritative for states that only it can directly establish.

Examples:

```text id="vq2q6l"
package registry → whether publication exists
cloud platform   → whether deployment is running
Git              → whether commit exists
CI provider      → native run conclusion
```

Monad may record these facts but does not manufacture them independently.

---

# Native reality

Where a property describes native reality, the native system's observed state is often the authoritative observation.

Example:

```text id="zdi3uz"
deployment.running
```

should generally come from the deployment platform.

A desired-state document cannot make a deployment running merely by stating that it should be.

---

# Desired-state authority

Desired state may have a different authority source.

Example:

```text id="76y5i0"
desired replicas = 3
```

could be authoritative in infrastructure configuration, while:

```text id="3jc58e"
observed replicas = 2
```

is authoritative in the runtime system.

Both can simultaneously be valid.

---

# Desired versus observed

Monad therefore preserves:

```text id="f4zuh9"
desired state
≠
observed state
```

Authority is resolved independently for each.

---

# Reconciliation direction

Reconciliation MUST use authority to determine permitted convergence direction.

Example:

```text id="il3u7o"
desired config authoritative
runtime drift observed
→ remediate runtime
```

Contrast:

```text id="pkl5yt"
native issue tracker authoritative for workflow state
Monad cache stale
→ update Monad
```

---

# Reconciliation must not invent authority

A reconciliation engine MUST NOT decide authority merely because it must choose some direction.

If authority is unresolved, reconciliation should surface the ambiguity.

---

# Bidirectional synchronization

Bidirectional synchronization is only legitimate where each overlapping field has:

* explicit authority,
* merge behavior,
* or conflict-resolution semantics.

---

# Write authority

An integration write requires authority for the operation.

Read access does not imply write authority.

---

# Mutation precondition

Consequential mutation SHOULD re-evaluate authority immediately before execution when the authorization context may have changed.

---

# Long-running operations

For long-running operations, policy must define whether authorization is evaluated:

* only at start,
* continuously,
* at consequential checkpoints,
* or again before commit.

---

# Approval

Approval records an authorized actor's affirmative governance act.

Approval should identify:

* approver,
* target,
* action,
* scope,
* revision,
* timestamp,
* governing policy.

---

# Approval is scoped

Approval for:

```text id="io4j1t"
release version 1.2.0
```

does not imply approval for:

```text id="fc8zez"
release version 1.3.0
```

---

# Approval expiration

Approvals MAY expire.

Policy should define expiration where underlying conditions can materially change.

---

# Approval invalidation

A change to the approved target MAY invalidate an approval.

Example:

```text id="5f51s5"
code changes after security approval
```

may require re-approval.

---

# Multiple approvals

A policy MAY require multiple independent approvals.

Example:

```text id="rfn4ge"
security approval
+
release approval
```

---

# Separation of duties

Monad SHOULD support policies requiring different actors to perform distinct governance steps.

Example:

```text id="scjt5e"
author
≠
approver
```

where appropriate.

---

# Self-approval

Self-approval MAY be permitted or forbidden according to policy.

It must not be assumed either way.

---

# Emergency authority

Emergency override mechanisms MAY exist.

They must be explicit and auditable.

---

# Break-glass operation

A break-glass authorization SHOULD capture:

* actor,
* reason,
* scope,
* time,
* operation,
* affected targets,
* follow-up review requirement.

---

# Emergency does not erase policy

Emergency authority temporarily overrides or bypasses normal rules according to a higher-level rule.

It does not make the normal rule disappear.

---

# Authority inheritance

Authority MAY be inherited through hierarchy.

Example:

```text id="nhudbl"
organization
  ↓
project
  ↓
repository
```

Inheritance rules must define how child scopes override or restrict parent authority.

---

# Inheritance must not be implicit

Hierarchy alone must not create unbounded authority unless policy says it does.

---

# Ownership

Ownership may inform authority but is not automatically equivalent to authority.

A code owner may be able to review a component but not approve a production release.

---

# Subject ownership

Monad MAY model Subject owners separately from authority grants.

---

# Authority groups

Authority may be granted to:

* teams,
* roles,
* groups,
* governance bodies.

Membership resolution must be time-aware when historical audits depend on it.

---

# Historical membership

To understand a past action, Monad may need to know:

```text id="mj2xxq"
Was actor A a member of team B at that time?
```

Current membership is insufficient.

---

# Historical authority

Authority decisions SHOULD be reconstructable historically.

A past action should not become retroactively unauthorized merely because roles changed later.

---

# Authority provenance

Authority records must preserve origin.

Examples:

```text id="8ocn80"
defined in policy
delegated by owner
granted by governance vote
derived from repository ownership
imported from native provider
```

---

# Derived authority

Derived authority MAY be computed from authoritative inputs.

Example:

```text id="vy7zc4"
team membership
+
role assignment
+
policy
→ effective authority
```

The derivation must remain inspectable.

---

# Imported native permissions

Monad MAY import native provider permissions.

These are facts about provider authorization.

They must not be silently promoted to universal Monad governance authority.

---

# Provider-admin distinction

Being a provider administrator does not automatically grant authority over all project governance decisions.

---

# Root credentials

Root or owner credentials confer technical power.

They do not necessarily confer project-legitimate authority.

---

# Service accounts

Service accounts SHOULD have narrowly scoped machine authority and credentials.

---

# Credential rotation

Changing credentials must not change the semantic identity of the actor or authority grant.

---

# Impersonation

If a system allows acting on behalf of another principal, impersonation MUST be explicitly represented and auditable.

---

# Proxy execution

Example:

```text id="ktnkxl"
Human
  ↓ authorizes
Monad agent
  ↓ invokes
GitHub App
  ↓ mutates
GitHub
```

The audit chain must retain all relevant principals.

---

# Initiator versus executor

Monad distinguishes:

```text id="4qk6zx"
initiator
executor
native credential principal
approver
```

These may all be different actors.

---

# Causation

Authority provenance should connect the initiating intent to the consequential native mutation.

---

# Authority decision record

A consequential authority decision MAY be retained as:

```yaml id="qfx6cb"
id: AUTHD-0082

actor: actor:thomas
initiator: actor:thomas
executor: agent:monad-executor

operation:
  type: deployment.promote

target:
  subject: deployment:api-production
  revision: release:1.4.2

decision:
  result: allow

basis:
  - authority: AUTH-0021
  - policy: POL-0014
  - approval: APR-0039

evaluated_at: 2026-08-29T17:18:00Z
```

---

# Authority Evidence

Records used to establish effective authority MAY themselves require provenance.

Examples:

```text id="1arve8"
team membership
approval record
delegation
policy revision
native permission response
```

---

# Evidence does not replace policy

Evidence may prove that a condition is satisfied.

The policy defines why that condition matters.

---

# Policy revisions

Authority decisions must bind to the policy revision used for evaluation where consequential.

---

# Rule evolution

Changing an authority rule affects future evaluations unless the governing process explicitly applies changes retroactively.

---

# Retroactivity

Retroactive authority changes SHOULD be rare and explicit.

Historical records must preserve the rule actually applied at execution time.

---

# Authority snapshots

For consequential operations, Monad SHOULD retain sufficient context to reconstruct the authorization decision.

---

# Local-first operation

Authority evaluation SHOULD remain possible locally where the required governing state is locally available.

---

# Offline ambiguity

When authority requires fresh remote state that cannot be obtained, Monad MUST NOT silently assume authorization.

It should return:

```text id="m5dqkw"
unknown
```

or deny according to policy.

---

# Cached authority data

Cached membership or permission data must carry freshness.

---

# Stale authority state

Stale data MUST be distinguishable from current data.

---

# Sensitive authority information

Authority metadata may itself be sensitive.

Access to audit details MAY be separately governed.

---

# Confidential policies

Policies MAY contain restricted content.

Their existence and effective consequences can be modeled without exposing secret details to unauthorized actors.

---

# Multi-organization authority

Monad must support authority scoped to organizational boundaries.

An actor authorized in Organization A does not inherit authority in Organization B.

---

# Multi-project authority

Authority should not leak across projects merely because identities or integrations share infrastructure.

---

# Multi-account integration

Provider credentials associated with one account must not grant implicit authority over another configured account.

---

# Cross-environment safety

When an operation could target multiple similar environments, target resolution must be explicit before authorization.

```text id="bk2m7u"
api-staging
api-production
```

must not be conflated.

---

# Ambiguous target

A consequential action with an ambiguous target SHOULD be rejected rather than resolved by guesswork.

---

# Authority and identity

Authority attaches to stable identities.

Renaming a file or repository must not accidentally destroy or broaden authority.

---

# Authority and Representation migration

If a canonical Representation moves, its authority mapping should move through an explicit continuity event rather than being inferred from path similarity alone.

---

# Provider migration

When moving from one provider to another, authority handoff must be explicit.

Example:

```text id="sfoz32"
GitHub Issues authoritative until T1
Linear authoritative after T1
```

---

# Migration overlap

During migration, both providers may temporarily exist.

This does not mean both are equally authoritative.

---

# Authority handoff

A handoff MAY include:

* old authority source,
* new authority source,
* effective time,
* migration scope,
* validation,
* rollback rule.

---

# Authority revocation after migration

The previous source SHOULD be downgraded to historical or read-only authority once handoff completes.

---

# Read-only authority

A historical system may remain authoritative for historical facts even after losing authority over current mutable state.

---

# Historical truth versus current authority

Example:

```text id="he6xkk"
old issue tracker
```

may remain authoritative for:

```text id="7s17vs"
what its state was on 2026-08-01
```

while no longer defining current workflow state.

---

# Temporal authority

Authority MAY depend on valid time.

Example:

```text id="isya7w"
before migration:
GitHub owns workflow state

after migration:
new tracker owns workflow state
```

---

# Event time versus observation time

Historical authority evaluation should use the relevant effective time, not merely the time Monad observed the data.

---

# Authority conflicts are first-class

Monad MUST preserve unresolved authority conflict explicitly.

It must not rewrite it into ordinary value divergence.

---

# Conflict example

```text id="0ag8tg"
POL-001:
release manager may approve production release

POL-014:
security incident mode forbids all releases
```

The system needs precedence rules.

---

# Conflict categories

Authority conflicts MAY include:

```text id="s1ybvu"
allow-vs-deny
source-vs-source
policy-vs-policy
delegation-vs-revocation
native-permission-vs-Monad-policy
approval-vs-target-revision
constitutional-vs-local-rule
```

---

# Conflict resolution

Resolution must produce provenance.

Example:

```text id="v8g6pd"
POL-014 overrides POL-001 because incident-mode deny has higher precedence.
```

---

# Unresolved conflict

If policy cannot determine precedence, consequential action SHOULD be denied or held pending resolution.

---

# Authority dispute

Human governance may be required to resolve ambiguous or contradictory authority records.

---

# Resolution must not erase conflict history

After resolution, the previous conflict remains part of audit history.

---

# Authority review

High-consequence authority grants SHOULD be reviewable periodically.

Examples:

```text id="av93y2"
production deployment
security policy mutation
release signing
credential administration
```

---

# Orphaned authority

Monad SHOULD detect authority grants whose actors, roles, or target resources no longer exist.

---

# Overbroad authority

Monad SHOULD support analysis for grants broader than their observed usage or intended scope.

---

# Dormant authority

Unused authority MAY be reported for review.

---

# Expiring authority

Time-limited grants SHOULD be queryable before expiration.

---

# Privilege escalation

Changes that increase authority SHOULD themselves require appropriate authority.

---

# Authority mutation

Creating, changing, delegating, or revoking authority is itself a governed operation.

---

# Self-modification boundary

A component must not silently grant itself additional authority merely because it controls its persistence mechanism.

---

# AI self-escalation

An AI agent MUST NOT change its own authority unless an external governing policy and authorized actor explicitly permit that action.

---

# Prompt content is not authority

Instructions contained in external content are data.

They do not gain authority over AI execution because they resemble commands.

---

# Tool descriptions are not authority

A tool saying:

```text id="id6lmd"
this action can deploy production
```

describes capability.

It does not establish permission to do so.

---

# User request and authority

A user's request may serve as intent.

It does not automatically prove that the user has authority over every requested target.

---

# Confirmation

User confirmation MAY be one policy input.

Confirmation alone is not a universal authority model.

---

# Approval interface

Interfaces SHOULD show:

* target,
* environment,
* action,
* revision,
* consequence,
* authority basis,

before requesting consequential approval.

---

# Success does not prove authorization

A native system accepting an operation proves only that the native authorization layer permitted it.

It does not prove that Monad governance was satisfied.

---

# Unauthorized success

If an operation succeeds natively but is later discovered to have violated Monad authority rules, the native change remains real and must be handled as a governance incident.

---

# Failed authorized operation

An operation may be fully authorized and still fail technically.

Therefore:

```text id="fz4bhm"
authorized ≠ succeeded
```

---

# Audit model

Consequential audit records SHOULD answer:

```text id="s8tzjp"
Who initiated the action?

Who approved it?

Who executed it?

What authority rule permitted it?

Which policy revision applied?

What target and revision were covered?

What native credentials were used?

What happened?

Was the result verified?
```

---

# Audit immutability

Authority-decision history SHOULD be protected against silent rewriting.

Corrections should append or supersede previous records with provenance.

---

# Authority correction

If an authority record was incorrect, Monad should record the correction rather than pretending the original state never existed.

---

# Governance incident

Unauthorized or ambiguous consequential actions MAY become explicit governance incidents.

---

# Notification

Policies MAY require notification when:

* privileged authority is granted,
* authority is escalated,
* emergency override is used,
* authorization is denied repeatedly,
* revoked credentials remain active,
* authority conflicts appear.

---

```text id="pmmjnt"
Which authority handoffs are in progress?
```

Rejected.

Rejected.

Rejected.

# Progressive formalization

Authority can be implemented progressively.

## Stage 1 — Declared source ownership

Important properties identify their authoritative source.

---

## Stage 2 — Actor roles

Mutation permissions become role-aware.

---

## Stage 3 — Explicit authority records

Authority becomes machine-readable.

---

## Stage 4 — Policy evaluation

Authority is resolved through policy rather than application-specific branching.

---

## Stage 5 — Delegation

Time- and scope-bounded delegated authority becomes available.

---

## Stage 6 — Approval binding

Consequential operations bind approvals to exact targets and revisions.

---

## Stage 7 — Authority-aware reconciliation

Synchronization direction is governed explicitly.

---

## Stage 8 — Multi-system authority

Properties can be owned independently across native systems and Monad.

---

## Stage 9 — Historical reconstruction

Past authorization decisions become reproducible from retained policy and authority state.

---

## Stage 10 — Governed autonomous execution

AI and automation can act autonomously within explicit, auditable authority envelopes.

---

# Alternatives considered

## Alternative A — One global source of truth

Rejected.

It does not reflect the distributed nature of engineering reality.

---

## Alternative B — Native system always authoritative

Rejected.

Native systems frequently contain projections, caches, generated state, or merely operational views of semantically governed artifacts.

---

## Alternative C — Monad always authoritative

Rejected.

Monad must not pretend it owns facts established only by native systems.

---

## Alternative D — Last writer wins

Rejected.

This confuses chronology with legitimacy.

---

## Alternative E — Hard-coded ownership rules

Rejected as the long-term architecture.

Implementation defaults may exist, but governed authority must become inspectable and evolvable.

---

## Alternative F — Provider permissions define authority

Rejected.

Provider permission systems model provider access, not the full governance of Monad.

---

# Consequences

## Positive

This decision provides:

* precise reconciliation direction,
* safer automation,
* meaningful AI boundaries,
* property-level source ownership,
* explicit approval semantics,
* provider-independent governance,
* historical auditability,
* controlled authority migration,
* and a clean separation between technical capability and legitimate action.

---

## Negative

The authority model adds:

* policy complexity,
* more metadata,
* additional evaluation steps,
* explicit conflict handling,
* governance configuration,
* and a larger audit surface.

This complexity is intentional because consequential automation without explicit authority is unsafe and difficult to reason about.

# Acceptance criteria

## Property-level authority

One Subject can use different authoritative sources for different properties.

---

## Native permission distinction

An actor with provider write access can still be denied by Monad governance.

---

## Monad permission distinction

An actor recognized as authorized by Monad can still fail because native provider permission is absent.

---

## Target revision

Approval for revision A does not automatically authorize revision B.

---

## Environment scope

Staging deployment authority does not authorize production deployment.

---

## Delegation

A delegate cannot acquire authority broader than the delegator's applicable scope.

---

## Revocation

Revoked authority remains visible historically but cannot authorize future operations.

---

## Reconciliation

A divergence can be resolved in the correct direction based on declared property authority.

---

## Unknown state

Insufficient authority information does not silently produce an allow result.

---

## AI operation

AI tool access alone does not authorize a consequential native mutation.

---

## Migration

Authority over a property can move from one provider to another without changing Subject identity.

---

## Historical audit

A past operation can identify the policy revision and authority basis used at the time.

---

# Governing principle

> **Authority in Monad is explicit, scoped, contextual, and auditable. No system, actor, credential, Representation, or provider becomes authoritative merely because it can store, observe, or change state.**

The architecture must always preserve the distinction:

```text id="2ipb15"
what exists
what is observed
what is desired
what is allowed
who may decide
who may act
what actually happened
```

These may originate from different places.

Monad's job is not to collapse them into one vague "source of truth."

Its job is to make their relationships explicit enough that humans, software, and AI can act without silently crossing authority boundaries.
