# ADR-0005 — Claim, Evidence, Provenance, and Verification Model

**Status:** Accepted
**Decision type:** Foundational architecture
**Requirements baseline:** RB-001
**Traceability artifact:** RTM-001
**Depends on:** ADR-0001, ADR-0002, ADR-0003, ADR-0004
**Primary requirement families:** `REQ-PROV`, `REQ-EVD`, `REQ-VFY`
**Consequential consumers:** `REQ-AUTH`, `REQ-REC`, `REQ-INT`, `REQ-EXEC`, `REQ-AI`, `REQ-REL`

---

# Decision

Monad will represent **Claims, Evidence, Provenance, and Verification as distinct first-class concepts**.

They will not be collapsed into a generic:

```text
fact
```

or:

```text
status
```

field.

The conceptual chain is:

```text
Claim
  │
  │ supported / challenged by
  ▼
Evidence
  │
  │ originated / derived through
  ▼
Provenance

Expectation
  │
  │ evaluated using
  ▼
Verification Method
  │
  │ executes / inspects
  ▼
Observation
  │
  │ yields
  ▼
Evidence
  │
  │ supports
  ▼
Verification Result
```

A Claim states something.

Evidence bears on whether that Claim should be believed.

Provenance explains where the Evidence or Claim came from and how it was produced.

Verification evaluates an explicit expectation using a defined method and produces a result grounded in Evidence.

<Callout type="info" title="Core rule">
Monad must never treat an assertion as verified merely because it exists, an observation as Evidence merely because it was collected, or Evidence as conclusive merely because it supports a Claim.

Each epistemic transition must remain inspectable.
</Callout>

---

# Context

Modern engineering systems produce enormous quantities of assertions.

Examples include:

```text
"The build passed."

"The implementation satisfies REQ-042."

"This package was produced from commit abc123."

"The production service is healthy."

"The architecture follows ADR-0004."

"This issue is complete."

"This migration preserved all data."

"This AI-generated patch is correct."
```

Those statements vary dramatically in strength.

Some are:

* human assertions,
* provider status values,
* generated interpretations,
* test results,
* observations,
* approvals,
* inferred conclusions,
* or verified outcomes.

Without explicit epistemic modeling, they are easily flattened into labels such as:

```text
done
passed
verified
compliant
healthy
approved
```

That destroys the information needed to answer:

```text
Why should I believe this?

Who asserted it?

What Evidence supports it?

What Evidence contradicts it?

How fresh is the Evidence?

What exact revision was evaluated?

Which method was used?

Did the method actually run?

What did it observe?

Was the result deterministic?

Has the underlying state changed since?
```

---

# Problem

Monad must support reliable reasoning across:

* requirements,
* architecture decisions,
* source code,
* tests,
* build systems,
* deployments,
* documentation,
* generated artifacts,
* issue trackers,
* AI output,
* human reviews,
* operational telemetry,
* and external systems.

This requires a formal distinction between:

```text
what someone says
what was observed
what supports the assertion
where the support came from
what procedure evaluated it
what conclusion that procedure reached
```

Without that distinction, Monad cannot provide trustworthy traceability.

---

# Decision drivers

The model must support:

* human and machine assertions,
* explicit Evidence,
* competing Evidence,
* Evidence freshness,
* Evidence revision binding,
* provenance chains,
* deterministic and probabilistic evaluation,
* reproducible verification,
* AI-assisted reasoning,
* review,
* release readiness,
* reconciliation,
* auditability,
* historical reconstruction,
* local-first operation,
* and progressive formalization.

---

# Claim

A **Claim** is an assertion that some proposition is true, false, expected, believed, or otherwise asserted about a Subject.

Example:

```yaml
id: CLM-0042

subject: requirement:REQ-042

proposition:
  predicate: satisfied
  value: true

asserted_by: actor:developer
```

---

# Claims are not facts automatically

Monad MUST NOT treat a Claim as true merely because the Claim exists.

```text
Claim existence ≠ Claim truth
```

---

# Claim identity

Claims SHOULD have stable identity when they participate in:

* Evidence relationships,
* verification,
* review,
* contradiction,
* supersession,
* release decisions,
* or audit history.

---

# Claim subject

A Claim SHOULD identify what it is about.

The target may be:

* a Subject,
* a Representation,
* an Execution,
* a release,
* a deployment,
* another Claim,
* or a relationship.

---

# Claim proposition

The proposition MAY begin as prose.

Example:

```yaml
statement: >
  The generated lockfile corresponds to the current manifest.
```

More structured forms MAY later be introduced.

---

# Structured Claims

Where useful, a Claim MAY contain:

```yaml
predicate: satisfies
object: requirement:REQ-042
```

or:

```yaml
predicate: deployment.health
value: healthy
```

---

# Progressive Claim structure

Monad does not require every Claim to begin as formal logic.

The architecture supports progression from:

```text
opaque prose
    ↓
typed Claim
    ↓
predicate/value
    ↓
domain-specific structured assertion
```

without changing the conceptual role of Claim.

---

# Claim source

A Claim SHOULD retain who or what asserted it.

Possible sources include:

* human actor,
* automated system,
* integration,
* verification process,
* AI model,
* imported artifact,
* policy engine.

---

# Claim timestamp

A Claim SHOULD record when it was asserted where temporal interpretation matters.

---

# Claim scope

A Claim may be scoped to:

* revision,
* environment,
* platform,
* configuration,
* time interval,
* repository,
* package version,
* execution run,
* deployment,
* or other contextual boundary.

---

# Revision-bound Claims

A Claim about software correctness SHOULD normally bind to the revision it concerns.

Example:

```text
commit abc123 satisfies REQ-042
```

must not silently become:

```text
current main satisfies REQ-042
```

after later changes.

---

# Claim lifecycle

Claims MAY move through states such as:

```text
asserted
supported
challenged
verified
refuted
superseded
withdrawn
stale
```

These lifecycle states describe epistemic status.

They must not be confused with truth itself.

---

# Supported Claim

A Claim may be supported without being conclusively verified.

---

# Challenged Claim

Evidence MAY challenge a Claim without conclusively disproving it.

---

# Contradictory Claims

Monad MUST permit contradictory Claims to coexist.

Example:

```text
CLM-001:
deployment is healthy

CLM-002:
deployment is unhealthy
```

The system should represent the disagreement rather than immediately deleting one assertion.

---

# Claim supersession

A newer Claim MAY supersede an older one.

Supersession does not erase historical assertions.

---

# Claim correction

Corrections SHOULD preserve:

* original Claim,
* correcting Claim,
* relationship,
* reason,
* provenance.

---

# Evidence

**Evidence** is an inspectable artifact, observation, record, output, or other item that bears on a Claim.

---

# Evidence is distinct from Claim

Example:

```text
Claim:
"All unit tests pass."

Evidence:
test report from run 9182
```

The test report is not the Claim itself.

---

# Evidence need not support

Evidence MAY:

* support,
* challenge,
* refute,
* qualify,
* contextualize,
* or remain inconclusive toward a Claim.

---

# Evidence relationship

The relationship between Evidence and Claim SHOULD be explicit.

Example:

```yaml
claim: CLM-0042
evidence: EVD-0191
relation: supports
```

---

# Evidence strength

Evidence MAY carry an evaluation of strength.

Example:

```text
weak
moderate
strong
conclusive-within-defined-method
```

Monad must not assume every Evidence item is equally persuasive.

---

# Evidence type

Evidence MAY include:

* source code,
* test output,
* build logs,
* package digest,
* deployment observation,
* benchmark result,
* static-analysis report,
* review record,
* external provider response,
* screenshot,
* generated report,
* structured query result,
* telemetry,
* human inspection record.

---

# Evidence identity

Consequential Evidence SHOULD have stable identity.

---

# Evidence content

Evidence may contain:

* inline structured content,
* immutable content,
* content digest,
* external/native reference,
* artifact reference,
* or a combination.

---

# Evidence preservation

Evidence used for consequential decisions SHOULD remain inspectable for as long as the decision must remain auditable.

---

# Evidence immutability

Evidence SHOULD be append-oriented.

If Evidence content changes, the new content should normally become a new revision or Evidence record.

---

# Evidence revision

Evidence should bind to the exact revision or observed state from which it was produced.

---

# Evidence scope

Evidence SHOULD identify relevant scope such as:

* Subject,
* revision,
* execution,
* environment,
* platform,
* configuration,
* provider,
* time,
* test subset.

---

# Evidence freshness

Evidence can become stale.

Monad MUST therefore distinguish:

```text
valid when collected
```

from:

```text
still sufficiently fresh for this decision
```

---

# Freshness policy

Freshness requirements SHOULD be policy- or verification-specific.

Example:

```text
dependency-license scan:
acceptable age = 24 hours

immutable package digest:
may remain valid indefinitely
```

---

# Stale Evidence

Stale Evidence is not deleted.

It remains historical Evidence but may cease to satisfy a current decision requirement.

---

# Evidence validity

Evidence MAY become invalid because:

* source revision changed,
* environment changed,
* configuration changed,
* collection method was defective,
* provenance was corrupted,
* artifact expired,
* verification contract changed.

---

# Evidence invalidation

Invalidation MUST preserve why the Evidence became unusable.

---

# Missing Evidence

Missing required Evidence MUST NOT be interpreted as passing Evidence.

```text
missing ≠ pass
```

---

# Unavailable Evidence

Evidence may be unavailable without being nonexistent.

Example:

```text
permission denied
```

must not become:

```text
evidence does not exist
```

---

# Partial Evidence

Partially collected Evidence must be represented as incomplete.

---

# Evidence collection failure

Failure to collect Evidence is distinct from negative Evidence.

---

# Negative Evidence

Negative Evidence explicitly bears against a Claim.

Example:

```text
Claim:
service is reachable

Evidence:
three independent connection attempts failed
```

---

# Absence of Evidence

Absence of Evidence is not automatically Evidence of absence.

---

# Evidence aggregation

Multiple Evidence items MAY jointly support a Claim.

---

# Evidence conflict

Evidence items MAY conflict.

Example:

```text
unit tests pass
integration tests fail
```

The system must preserve both.

---

# Evidence weighting

Policies or verification methods MAY weight Evidence differently.

---

# Evidence source independence

Monad SHOULD be capable of distinguishing multiple Evidence items generated from the same underlying source.

Ten derived reports from one failing observation do not necessarily constitute ten independent pieces of Evidence.

---

# Provenance

**Provenance** records origin, custody, derivation, transformation, and contextual history relevant to interpreting a Claim, Evidence item, or artifact.

---

# Provenance is distinct from Evidence

Provenance answers:

```text
Where did this come from?
How was it produced?
What transformed it?
Under what context?
```

Evidence answers:

```text
What supports or challenges the Claim?
```

---

# Provenance chain

A provenance chain MAY include:

```text
source repository
      ↓
checkout
      ↓
build execution
      ↓
test execution
      ↓
test report
      ↓
Evidence record
      ↓
verification result
```

---

# Provenance source

Provenance SHOULD identify native source references where relevant.

---

# Provenance actor

Provenance MAY identify:

* author,
* executor,
* service principal,
* AI agent,
* reviewer,
* collector,
* importer.

---

# Provenance timestamps

Important provenance events SHOULD include time.

---

# Provenance revisions

Where versioned artifacts are involved, provenance MUST bind to stable revisions when consequential.

Examples:

```text
Git commit SHA
artifact digest
package version + digest
configuration revision
policy revision
```

---

# Provenance transformations

Transformations SHOULD be represented where they affect interpretation.

Example:

```text
source
→ transpilation
→ bundling
→ packaging
→ signing
```

---

# Derived Evidence

Evidence generated through transformation MUST retain derivation provenance.

---

# Provenance does not imply trust

Knowing where something came from does not prove it is correct.

```text
provenance ≠ trustworthiness
```

---

# Trust policy

A policy MAY use provenance as one input when deciding whether Evidence is acceptable.

---

# Chain gaps

Missing provenance links SHOULD be explicit.

The system should not fabricate continuity.

---

# Unknown provenance

Unknown provenance MUST be representable.

---

# Provenance correction

Provenance corrections must preserve prior recorded provenance and the correcting event.

---

# External provenance

External systems MAY provide native provenance such as:

* build IDs,
* commit SHAs,
* workflow IDs,
* package digests,
* deployment IDs,
* request IDs.

These should be preserved rather than normalized away.

---

# AI provenance

AI-produced artifacts or Claims SHOULD retain consequential inference context.

Relevant provenance may include:

* model/provider identity,
* model version where available,
* prompt or prompt digest,
* input artifact references,
* tool calls,
* generated output,
* runtime configuration,
* timestamp.

---

# AI context boundaries

AI provenance SHOULD capture enough context to reconstruct what information the model was allowed to use when the conclusion materially matters.

---

# Prompt confidentiality

Provenance requirements do not imply every prompt must be universally visible.

Sensitive context may be protected while retaining appropriate audit references.

---

# Verification

**Verification** is an explicit evaluation of an expectation against observed state using a defined method.

---

# Verification expectation

A Verification MUST identify what was expected.

Example:

```yaml
expectation:
  requirement: REQ-042
  predicate: satisfied
```

---

# Verification method

A Verification SHOULD identify how the expectation is evaluated.

Examples:

```text
unit test
integration test
schema validation
static analysis
manual review
artifact digest comparison
runtime probe
policy evaluation
formal proof
AI-assisted assessment
```

---

# Verification target

A Verification MUST identify the target it evaluated.

---

# Verification revision

Where mutable artifacts are involved, a Verification MUST bind to the evaluated revision.

---

# Verification execution

Where the method requires execution, Monad MUST distinguish:

```text
verification definition
```

from:

```text
verification execution
```

---

# Verification result

A Verification execution produces a result.

Possible results may include:

```text
pass
fail
indeterminate
error
skipped
not-applicable
```

---

# Error is not failure

A method error is not automatically evidence that the expectation is false.

Example:

```text
test runner crashed
```

is different from:

```text
test assertion failed
```

---

# Skipped is not pass

```text
skipped ≠ pass
```

---

# Indeterminate is first-class

A Verification MUST be allowed to conclude that available Evidence is insufficient.

---

# Verification result Evidence

A Verification result SHOULD identify the Evidence on which it depends.

---

# Pass Evidence

A pass result must not exist solely as an ungrounded Boolean where inspection or reproducibility matters.

---

# Failure Evidence

A failure result should retain the observations that caused failure.

---

# Verification Evidence chain

A useful chain is:

```text
Requirement
   ↓
Expectation
   ↓
Verification Method
   ↓
Execution
   ↓
Observation
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Verification Result
```

---

# Verification definition versus result

A test existing in the repository is not the same as that test having passed.

```text
verification method exists
≠
verification passed
```

---

# Implementation versus satisfaction

The existence of implementation code does not prove requirement satisfaction.

---

# Requirement traceability

Requirement satisfaction SHOULD be supported by explicit Verification relationships.

Example:

```text
REQ-042
   ↓ verified by
VFY-019
   ↓ produced
EVD-082
```

---

# Multi-method verification

One expectation MAY require several verification methods.

Example:

```text
security requirement
├── static analysis
├── integration test
├── configuration inspection
└── human review
```

---

# Composite verification

A composite result SHOULD preserve component outcomes.

---

# Verification policy

Policy MAY define which methods and Evidence are sufficient for a particular requirement.

---

# Verification confidence

Verification MAY carry confidence or certainty semantics where the method is inherently probabilistic.

---

# Deterministic verification

A deterministic method SHOULD report deterministic semantics where applicable.

Example:

```text
SHA-256 digest equals expected digest
```

---

# Probabilistic verification

AI-based evaluation, heuristics, statistical tests, and sampling MAY be probabilistic.

They MUST NOT be represented as deterministic merely for UI simplicity.

---

# AI verification

An AI evaluation MAY contribute to verification.

It should identify:

* model,
* relevant context,
* evaluation rubric,
* input revisions,
* output,
* confidence where meaningful,
* supporting Evidence.

---

# AI evaluation is not universal proof

An AI saying:

```text
This implementation satisfies REQ-042.
```

is a Claim unless a verification contract explicitly defines the AI evaluation itself as an accepted verification method.

---

# Verification contracts

A verification contract defines:

* expectation,
* target scope,
* method,
* required inputs,
* acceptance criteria,
* Evidence requirements,
* freshness,
* acceptable uncertainty,
* result semantics.

---

# Verification reproducibility

Verification SHOULD be reproducible when technically feasible.

---

# Reproducibility context

Reproducibility may require preserving:

* source revision,
* dependency lock,
* tool versions,
* environment,
* configuration,
* operating system,
* architecture,
* seed,
* external service revisions.

---

# Non-reproducible methods

Some verification methods are inherently non-reproducible.

Example:

```text
live production observation
```

Such results remain valid historical observations when provenance is sufficient.

---

# Verification timestamp

A Verification result MUST record when the evaluation occurred where time matters.

---

# Verification freshness

A Verification result may become stale when its target changes.

---

# Change invalidation

A consequential change to an evaluated artifact SHOULD invalidate or mark stale affected Verification results.

---

# Impact analysis

Monad SHOULD eventually identify which verification results may be invalidated by a given change.

---

# Rerun

Rerunning Verification creates new execution history.

It MUST NOT overwrite prior results.

---

# Latest result

The most recent Verification result may be operationally useful.

It does not erase older results.

---

# Flaky verification

Repeated inconsistent results SHOULD be detectable.

---

# Flakiness is Evidence

Flakiness itself may become Evidence about test reliability or system nondeterminism.

---

# Verification method reliability

Monad MAY track reliability of verification methods.

---

# False positive risk

A Verification contract MAY document expected false-positive risk.

---

# False negative risk

A Verification contract MAY document expected false-negative risk.

---

# Sampling

Sample-based Verification MUST make its sampling strategy explicit when consequential.

---

# Manual verification

Human inspection MAY be a legitimate Verification method.

It should retain:

* reviewer,
* method/checklist,
* target revision,
* timestamp,
* observations,
* result.

---

# Review versus Verification

Review and Verification may overlap but are not identical.

A review can contain:

* comments,
* recommendations,
* discussion,
* approval.

Only explicit evaluation against an expectation constitutes Verification.

---

# Approval versus Verification

Approval answers:

```text
May we proceed?
```

Verification answers:

```text
Did the defined expectation hold?
```

Approval MUST NOT be used as Evidence that a requirement passed unless the verification contract explicitly says the approval itself is the required condition.

---

# Evidence versus approval

An approval record can be Evidence that approval occurred.

It is not automatically Evidence that the approved artifact is technically correct.

---

# Execution versus Verification

Execution changes or interrogates real systems.

Verification evaluates expectations.

An Execution may be part of Verification.

They remain distinct.

---

# Successful execution versus successful verification

Example:

```text
deployment command succeeded
```

does not imply:

```text
deployed service satisfies health requirement
```

A subsequent Verification may be required.

---

# API acceptance versus Verification

A provider returning:

```text
202 Accepted
```

means the operation was accepted.

It does not verify the desired outcome.

---

# Observation

An observation records what was perceived through an inspection mechanism.

---

# Observation versus Evidence

An observation may become Evidence when it is retained and linked to a Claim or Verification.

---

# Observation time

Observation MUST distinguish:

```text
event time
observation time
```

where the difference matters.

---

# Observation source

Observation SHOULD identify the system or method used.

---

# Observation completeness

Observations may be:

```text
complete
partial
sampled
filtered
unknown
```

---

# Cached observations

Cached observations MUST be distinguishable from live observations.

---

# Verification and authority

Verification does not create authority.

A passing Verification may be required by policy before an authorized operation, but the Verification itself does not authorize that operation.

---

# Verification and reconciliation

Reconciliation may use Verification to determine whether convergence succeeded.

---

# Reconciliation result versus Verification

Agreement between two systems is not automatically Verification of correctness.

---

# Release readiness

A release-ready Claim SHOULD be supported by exact Verification results and Evidence for the release candidate revision.

Example:

```text
release:1.8.0
  ├── requirement verification
  ├── security verification
  ├── build provenance
  ├── artifact digest
  └── deployment-readiness checks
```

---

# Release mutation

Changing the release candidate revision invalidates revision-bound readiness conclusions unless the verification contract explicitly permits reuse.

---

# Build provenance

A release artifact SHOULD be traceable to the exact source and build process that produced it.

---

# Artifact digest

Immutable artifact digests provide strong Evidence for artifact identity.

They do not independently prove correctness.

---

# Test report

A test report is Evidence about the test execution.

It does not automatically prove that:

* the tests were sufficient,
* the correct revision was tested,
* the environment was representative,
* or every Requirement was covered.

---

# Coverage

Coverage metrics are Evidence.

They are not equivalent to correctness.

---

# Static analysis

Static-analysis success is Evidence within the analyzer's defined capability.

It is not universal verification.

---

# Formal proof

A formal proof can provide stronger Verification within its formal model.

The result still depends on the relationship between the model and the real implementation.

---

# Benchmark Evidence

Benchmark results must bind to:

* implementation revision,
* benchmark definition,
* environment,
* workload,
* configuration.

---

# Performance Claim

A performance Claim without those contextual boundaries is usually too ambiguous to verify reliably.

---

# Operational Evidence

Telemetry MAY support Claims about runtime behavior.

---

# Telemetry sampling

Sampled telemetry must not be treated as complete observation unless the method justifies that interpretation.

---

# Logs

Logs may be Evidence.

Logs are not automatically complete records of reality.

---

# Screenshots

Screenshots may be Evidence for visual or contextual observations.

They should retain origin and time when consequential.

---

# User reports

User reports are Claims or Evidence depending on context.

A report that:

```text
"The button does not work."
```

is primarily a Claim.

A recording showing the failure may become Evidence supporting it.

---

# Imported provider status

A CI provider's:

```text
success
```

is a native Claim/result about its run.

Monad should preserve the native semantics and provenance before deriving stronger conclusions.

---

# Derived Claims

Monad MAY derive Claims from Evidence.

Example:

```text
all required checks passed
→ release readiness supported
```

The derivation rule must remain inspectable.

---

# Derived verification result

Composite verification MAY derive a result from child results.

The derivation logic should be explicit.

---

# Claim dependencies

A Claim MAY depend on other Claims.

Example:

```text
release ready
```

may depend on:

```text
requirements satisfied
security review complete
artifacts built
deployment plan valid
```

---

# Circular Claim support

Monad SHOULD detect unsupported circular reasoning where possible.

Example:

```text
A is true because B
B is true because A
```

without independent Evidence.

---

# Evidence duplication

Duplicate copies of the same underlying Evidence SHOULD NOT automatically increase evidentiary strength.

---

# Evidence lineage

Monad SHOULD be able to identify common lineage among derived Evidence.

---

# Provenance graph

Conceptually:

```text
Source
  ↓
Representation
  ↓
Transformation
  ↓
Execution
  ↓
Observation
  ↓
Evidence
  ↓
Claim
  ↓
Decision
```

Each edge contributes context without collapsing the entities.

---

# Epistemic status

Monad MAY compute an epistemic status for Claims.

Example:

```text
unsubstantiated
supported
strongly-supported
contested
verified-under-contract
refuted
stale
```

The status must be derived from explicit rules rather than arbitrary UI labels.

---

# Truth is not stored as a magical Boolean

Monad SHOULD avoid a universal:

```yaml
truth: true
```

field that pretends uncertainty, scope, time, and Evidence do not exist.

---

# Current best understanding

Monad MAY expose a convenient current interpretation.

That interpretation must remain traceable to the Claims, Evidence, provenance, and verification behind it.

---

# Epistemic conflict

Conflicting supported Claims should produce an explicit epistemic conflict rather than silent winner selection.

---

# Recency

More recent Evidence MAY be more relevant.

Recency alone does not establish correctness.

---

# Authority of assertion

An authoritative actor may issue a Claim.

That gives the Claim governance significance.

It does not automatically make the proposition factually correct.

---

# Trusted source

A trusted source may raise default confidence.

Trust does not eliminate the need for Evidence where Verification is required.

---

# Source reputation

Monad MAY eventually model reliability characteristics of Evidence sources.

---

# Retraction

A Claim or Evidence source MAY retract a prior assertion.

Retraction must preserve historical provenance.

---

# Tamper Evidence

Consequential Evidence MAY include integrity protection such as:

* digest,
* signature,
* immutable storage reference,
* provider-native checksum.

---

# Signature semantics

A valid signature proves something about signing identity and content integrity according to the signature mechanism.

It does not automatically prove correctness of the signed content.

---

# Evidence integrity

Evidence integrity and evidentiary relevance are distinct.

---

# Evidence accessibility

Evidence MAY exist but become inaccessible.

The system should distinguish inaccessible from deleted.

---

# Evidence retention

Retention policy SHOULD consider:

* audit needs,
* release lifetime,
* regulatory constraints,
* cost,
* provider expiration,
* reproducibility.

---

# Expired external Evidence

If a native provider removes an artifact because of retention limits, Monad should preserve enough metadata to show that the Evidence once existed and is now unavailable.

---

# Evidence localization

Monad MAY cache or preserve critical Evidence locally where permitted.

---

# Local-first Verification

Verification that depends only on locally available state SHOULD remain executable locally.

---

# Remote Verification

Verification requiring native-system state must preserve integration and observation provenance.

---

# Offline state

When required Evidence cannot be refreshed offline, Monad should represent the resulting uncertainty explicitly.

---

# Security

Evidence and provenance can contain:

* secrets,
* credentials,
* personal data,
* internal paths,
* infrastructure details.

The model MUST support redaction and access control.

---

# Redaction

Redaction SHOULD preserve the fact that information existed while hiding unauthorized content.

---

# Redaction provenance

Security-sensitive redaction MAY itself be represented as a transformation.

---

# Secret leakage

Evidence collection MUST NOT require indiscriminate persistence of secrets.

---

# AI prompt injection

Externally sourced Evidence is untrusted data.

Instructions embedded in external Evidence MUST NOT automatically become AI execution instructions.

---

# AI-generated Evidence

AI-generated summaries or interpretations should normally be treated as derived Claims or derived Evidence with provenance to the underlying sources.

---

# AI summary is not source Evidence

A summary of a log does not replace the log where the original Evidence is required.

---

# AI citation

AI conclusions SHOULD link back to inspectable supporting Evidence when used consequentially.

---

# Human judgment

Human judgment may be required when Evidence is ambiguous.

Such judgment should become an explicit Claim, review, Verification, or decision rather than disappearing into undocumented interpretation.

---

# Verification governance

Policies MAY define which actors may:

* define verification methods,
* approve verification contracts,
* waive verification,
* accept failed verification,
* accept stale Evidence.

---

# Verification waiver

A waiver does not turn a failed or missing Verification into a pass.

It records that governance permitted proceeding despite the unmet verification condition.

---

# Waiver modeling

Example:

```yaml
verification:
  result: fail

waiver:
  approved: true
  reason: emergency release
```

not:

```yaml
verification:
  result: pass
```

---

# Exception

A policy exception is a governance artifact.

It must not rewrite historical Evidence.

---

# Verification override

Manual override SHOULD be represented separately from the original Verification result.

---

# Requirement satisfaction

Monad SHOULD distinguish:

```text
requirement asserted satisfied
requirement supported as satisfied
requirement verified as satisfied
requirement accepted despite failed verification
```

---

# Traceability

A requirement trace SHOULD eventually support traversal such as:

```text
Requirement
  ↓
Claim of satisfaction
  ↓
Verification
  ↓
Verification execution
  ↓
Evidence
  ↓
Provenance
  ↓
source revision / runtime observation
```

---

# Decision Evidence

ADRs MAY reference Evidence supporting a decision.

The ADR remains a governance decision.

The Evidence remains epistemic support.

---

# Decision invalidation

New Evidence may undermine an accepted ADR's assumptions without automatically revoking the ADR.

Governance determines what happens next.

---

# Hypotheses

Hypotheses are Claims explicitly marked as uncertain and intended for evaluation.

---

# Experiment

An experiment can produce Evidence used to evaluate a hypothesis.

---

# Hypothesis result

A failed hypothesis is not a failed project operation.

It is a successful epistemic outcome if the experiment validly provided informative Evidence.

---

# Experiment provenance

Experimental Evidence SHOULD retain:

* hypothesis,
* method,
* variables,
* environment,
* inputs,
* outputs,
* evaluator,
* revision.

---

# Reproducibility status

Experiment or Verification records MAY state whether results were independently reproduced.

---

# Independent corroboration

Independent Evidence MAY increase confidence.

Its independence should be justified by provenance rather than assumed.

---

# Historical reconstruction

Monad SHOULD make it possible to determine:

```text
What did we believe at time T?

Which Claims supported that belief?

Which Evidence was available?

What was its provenance?

Which verification results existed?

Which later Evidence changed our understanding?
```

---

# Time-aware Claims

A Claim may be valid for one period and false later.

Example:

```text
production deployment healthy at 12:00
```

does not imply health at 15:00.

---

# Current truth projection

A current-state projection may select the most relevant active Claims and Evidence.

The underlying history remains intact.

---

# Unknown

Monad MUST preserve unknown states.

```text
unknown ≠ false
```

---

# Inconclusive

Inconclusive verification is a legitimate result.

---

# Confidence

Confidence MAY be represented separately from result.

Example:

```yaml
result: pass
confidence: 0.71
```

only when the method meaningfully supports probabilistic interpretation.

---

# False precision

Probabilistic methods MUST NOT invent numerical confidence where the underlying method does not justify it.

---

# Claim confidence

Claim confidence MAY derive from:

* Evidence strength,
* source reliability,
* number of independent Evidence paths,
* verification results,
* freshness,
* contradiction.

---

# Evidence confidence

Confidence does not replace Evidence.

---

# Multi-repository verification

Verification MAY span multiple repositories.

Each participating revision must be identified.

---

# Multi-system verification

Verification MAY depend on several native systems.

Example:

```text
source repository revision
+
CI run
+
artifact registry digest
+
deployment observation
```

---

# Cross-system consistency

Cross-system agreement MAY be Evidence.

It does not automatically prove correctness.

---

# Reconciliation Evidence

A reconciliation run MAY generate Evidence describing:

* compared states,
* divergence,
* authority basis,
* action taken,
* resulting state.

---

# Reconciliation is not Verification automatically

A successful reconciliation establishes convergence according to its model.

A separate Verification may still be required to establish the desired system property.

---

# Execution Evidence

Execution records MAY become Evidence.

Example:

```text
migration execution completed
```

is Evidence that the operation ran.

It is not sufficient alone to prove that all data was preserved.

---

# Post-execution Verification

Consequential executions SHOULD support post-execution Verification when successful command completion is insufficient.

---

# Deployment example

```text
Execution:
deployment requested

Evidence:
provider accepted deployment

Observation:
deployment reached ready state

Verification:
health checks passed

Claim:
release successfully deployed
```

These are related but distinct records.

---

# Package example

```text
Claim:
package 1.4.0 corresponds to commit abc123

Evidence:
build provenance record
+
registry digest

Verification:
published digest == locally produced digest
```

---

# Requirements example

```text
Claim:
REQ-AUTH-042 is satisfied

Evidence:
policy engine contract tests

Verification:
VFY-AUTH-018 passes against commit abc123

Provenance:
repository + commit + test runner + environment
```

# AI review example

```yaml
claim:
  statement: >
    Implementation appears consistent with REQ-REC-042.

asserted_by:
```

## Claim equals fact

Rejected.

---

## Boolean verified field

Example:

```yaml
verified: true
```

with no method, target revision, Evidence, or timestamp.

Rejected for consequential use.

---

## Test exists therefore requirement is verified

Rejected.

---

## Test passed once therefore permanently verified

Rejected.

---

## Build success equals correctness

Rejected.

---

## Deployment accepted equals deployment healthy

Rejected.

---

## Issue closed equals requirement satisfied

Rejected.

---

## Approval equals technical verification

Rejected.

---

## Reviewer said LGTM equals proof

Rejected unless explicitly defined as the governing verification condition.

---

## AI said it is correct

Rejected as deterministic proof.

---

## Latest Evidence wins automatically

Rejected.

---

## More Evidence always means more confidence

Rejected.

Duplicate derivations may share one underlying source.

---

## Missing Evidence equals negative Evidence

Rejected.

---

## Evidence unavailable equals Evidence absent

Rejected.

---

## Provider status equals universal truth

Rejected.

---

## Log exists therefore event occurred exactly as interpreted

Rejected.

---

## Provenance proves correctness

Rejected.

---

## Signature proves correctness

Rejected.

---

## Digest proves semantic correctness

Rejected.

---

## Current Claim overwrites historical Claim

Rejected.

---

## Rerun overwrites previous verification

Rejected.

---

## Failed collector equals failed expectation

Rejected.

---

## Skipped test equals pass

Rejected.

## Timeout equals failure of target

Rejected.

---

## Reconciliation success equals system correctness

Rejected.

## Execution success equals Verification success

Rejected.

Rejected.

# Progressive formalization

## Stage 1 — Assertions and references

Capture important Claims and their supporting artifact references.

---

## Stage 2 — First-class Evidence

Create stable Evidence objects linked to Claims.

---

## Stage 3 — Provenance chains

Record origin, revision, execution, and transformation context.

---

## Stage 4 — Verification definitions

Make expectations and verification methods explicit.

---

## Stage 5 — Verification executions

Separate method definitions from individual runs.

---

## Stage 6 — Freshness and invalidation

Invalidate or stale verification when targets or methods change.

---

## Stage 7 — Composite reasoning

Derive higher-level Claims from multiple verification results and Evidence paths.

---

## Stage 8 — Confidence and conflict

Represent contradictory Claims, probabilistic methods, and Evidence strength.

---

## Stage 9 — Automated impact analysis

Identify which Claims and verification results may be affected by a change.

---

## Stage 10 — Governed machine reasoning

Humans and AI can make inspectable conclusions over a traceable epistemic graph without collapsing inference into undocumented "facts."

---

# Alternatives considered

## Alternative A — Generic fact model

Rejected.

It hides who asserted the proposition and why it should be believed.

---

## Alternative B — Boolean verification fields

Rejected.

They cannot retain method, revision, Evidence, freshness, or provenance.

---

## Alternative C — Treat test results as Claims only

Rejected.

It loses the important role of Evidence and explicit verification contracts.

---

## Alternative D — Treat every provider status as Evidence and truth

Rejected.

Provider semantics differ and observations are contextual.

---

## Alternative E — Use provenance only

Rejected.

Knowing origin does not explain whether an artifact supports a Claim.

---

## Alternative F — Use Evidence only

Rejected.

Evidence without Claims and explicit expectations lacks evaluative context.

---

## Alternative G — Treat AI conclusions as ordinary verified facts

Rejected.

AI evaluation remains probabilistic unless accepted through an explicit verification contract.

---

# Consequences

## Positive

This model enables:

* trustworthy requirement traceability,
* inspectable reasoning,
* reproducible verification,
* explicit uncertainty,
* stronger release gates,
* historical epistemic reconstruction,
* Evidence freshness,
* AI provenance,
* competing Claims,
* failure diagnosis,
* and future automated reasoning.

---

## Negative

The model requires:

* more entities,
* more relationships,
* more provenance capture,
* explicit invalidation rules,
* storage for Evidence,
* method definitions,
* and careful distinction between epistemic states.

This is intentional.

A system intended to reason about engineering reality cannot safely compress:

```text
assertion
observation
support
origin
evaluation
conclusion
```

into one Boolean.

# Acceptance criteria

## Claim independence

A Claim can exist without being accepted as true.

---

## Contradictory Claims

Two contradictory Claims may coexist without destructive overwrite.

---

## Evidence linkage

Evidence can explicitly support or challenge a Claim.

---

## Provenance lineage

An Evidence record can be traced to the source revision and execution that generated it.

---

## Freshness

A once-valid Evidence item can become stale without disappearing from history.

---

## Missing Evidence

A verification requiring unavailable Evidence does not produce a false pass.

---

## Error distinction

Verification-method failure is distinguishable from expectation failure.

---

## Revision binding

A Verification result for commit A does not automatically apply to commit B.

---

## Rerun history

Rerunning the same method creates a new execution/result rather than overwriting the previous one.

---

## AI evaluation

AI output remains a Claim or probabilistic evaluation unless an explicit verification contract recognizes it as a verification method.

---

## Approval distinction

Approval to proceed does not convert failed Verification into passing Verification.

---

## Release traceability

A release-readiness conclusion can identify the exact verification results and Evidence supporting it.

---

# Governing principle

> **Monad must preserve the difference between what is asserted, what is observed, what supports an assertion, where that support came from, how an expectation was evaluated, and what conclusion that evaluation justified.**

The epistemic model therefore preserves:

```text
Claim
Evidence
Provenance
Observation
Verification Method
Verification Execution
Verification Result
Confidence
Freshness
Scope
Revision
```

as related but non-interchangeable concepts.

The objective is not to turn engineering reality into a pile of unquestionable facts.

It is to make the basis of belief inspectable.

That is what allows humans, automation, and AI to reason about the system without silently transforming:

```text
someone said so
```

into:

```text
therefore it is true
```
