# ADR-0010 — AI Interaction, Delegation, and Consequential Action Model

**Status:** Accepted
**Decision type:** Foundational architecture
**Requirements baseline:** RB-001
**Traceability artifact:** RTM-001
**Depends on:** ADR-0001 through ADR-0009
**Primary requirement family:** REQ-AI
**Consequential consumers:** REQ-AUTH, REQ-GOV, REQ-EXEC, REQ-INT, REQ-PROV, REQ-EVD, REQ-VFY, REQ-REC, REQ-REPRO

---

## Decision

Monad SHALL treat AI as a **governed reasoning and delegation participant**, not as an inherently authoritative actor.

AI MAY participate throughout the engineering system by inspecting state, retrieving information, reasoning, inferring, hypothesizing, recommending, planning, generating, transforming, evaluating, requesting capability invocation, and executing capabilities that have been explicitly authorized.

These forms of participation SHALL remain semantically distinguishable.

The presence of an AI system, model, agent, tool, credential, integration, or executable capability SHALL NOT itself establish authority to perform a consequential action.

Consequential AI action SHALL be governed through explicit:

* identity,
* delegation,
* authority,
* policy,
* capability boundaries,
* target resolution,
* preconditions,
* provenance,
* execution records,
* post-action observation,
* Evidence,
* reconciliation,
* and verification.

The architectural flow is:

```text
engineering state
      │
      ▼
AI observation context
      │
      ▼
reasoning / inference
      │
      ├── Claim
      ├── hypothesis
      ├── recommendation
      ├── plan
      └── proposed action
      │
      ▼
authority + policy evaluation
      │
      ▼
governed capability
      │
      ▼
execution
      │
      ▼
observation
      │
      ▼
Evidence
      │
      ▼
verification / reconciliation
```

Monad SHALL preserve the boundaries between these stages rather than collapsing them into a single concept such as "the AI did it."

AI autonomy MAY exist, including autonomous execution without per-operation human approval, but only where bounded authority has been explicitly established through governance and delegation.

Autonomy SHALL therefore mean:

> execution within an explicitly governed scope

and SHALL NOT mean:

> absence of governance.

---

## Context

AI systems can participate in engineering work at nearly every layer of a modern software system.

They can:

* read repositories,
* inspect requirements,
* search documentation,
* interpret logs,
* classify artifacts,
* reason about architecture,
* generate code,
* produce migrations,
* propose configuration,
* execute commands,
* invoke external APIs,
* modify resources,
* create or update work items,
* deploy systems,
* evaluate results,
* and participate in verification.

This breadth makes AI materially different from a conventional passive tool.

At the same time, AI systems are probabilistic, context-dependent, susceptible to stale or adversarial information, and capable of operating through credentials and integrations whose technical power may exceed the authority legitimately delegated to the AI.

A naïve architecture can therefore accidentally collapse several distinct concepts:

```text
AI can see resource
→ AI understands resource
→ AI may modify resource
→ AI is authorized to modify resource
→ modification was correct
→ modification succeeded
→ intended effect occurred
→ result is verified
```

None of those implications is universally valid.

Monad already establishes architectural distinctions between:

```text
Authority ≠ Capability

Capability ≠ Credential

Execution ≠ Verification

Execution success ≠ Requirement satisfaction

Evidence ≠ Verification

Verification ≠ Approval

Approval ≠ Authority

Desired state ≠ Observed state

Modeled state ≠ Native reality
```

AI participation must preserve these distinctions.

The AI architecture must also integrate with the execution and reproducibility model established by ADR-0009. Consequential AI operations are engineering executions whose provenance, environment, inputs, model identity, tool invocation, outputs, and resulting effects may need to be reconstructed and challenged later.

---

## Problem

Monad requires an architecture that permits AI to reason broadly and act usefully without allowing probabilistic inference, contextual persuasion, model output, credentials, or tool availability to silently become system authority.

Without an explicit architecture, several failure modes become likely.

An AI may possess credentials that technically permit a privileged mutation even though no legitimate authority was delegated for that mutation.

An AI may infer a target incorrectly and apply an otherwise valid operation to the wrong repository, environment, account, branch, project, or production resource.

An AI may act on stale observations after engineering state changes between planning and execution.

External content may contain prompt-injection instructions that attempt to alter governance or redirect tool use.

A successful API response may be interpreted as proof that the desired semantic state was achieved.

Generated content may be treated as authoritative simply because a sophisticated model produced it.

An AI may evaluate its own output and have that evaluation incorrectly treated as independent Verification.

A model alias may change behavior while records continue to imply that the same model performed equivalent reasoning.

An autonomous loop may continue indefinitely, consume excessive resources, repeatedly mutate state, or amplify an initial mistake.

An AI may modify the policy governing its own authority and subsequently use the newly created permission.

A multi-agent system may obscure who proposed, authorized, executed, or verified an operation.

These failures cannot be solved merely by adding a generic "human in the loop."

The architecture must instead represent and govern the complete path from observation through reasoning, authorization, execution, Evidence, and verification.

---

## Decision drivers

The architecture is driven by the following requirements.

### Preserve authority semantics

Technical capability must not be mistaken for governed authority.

### Support useful autonomy

Monad must permit bounded autonomous AI operation where explicitly authorized rather than requiring manual approval for every operation.

### Preserve provenance

Consequential AI activity must remain reconstructable.

### Resist prompt injection

Information retrieved from external or native systems must remain data unless it enters through a trusted instruction channel.

### Prevent target ambiguity

Consequential operations must resolve their target explicitly.

### Prevent stale-plan execution

Planning and execution must remain distinct events with revalidation where state may have changed materially.

### Integrate with native systems

AI must operate through governed integrations and capabilities rather than bypassing native-system semantics.

### Preserve uncertainty

Inference, hypothesis, estimation, recommendation, observation, and known facts must remain distinguishable.

### Support independent verification

AI execution and AI evaluation must not automatically constitute Verification.

### Support multiple AI architectures

The design must apply to assistants, agents, autonomous loops, multi-agent systems, local models, hosted models, and future AI execution environments.

### Support reproducibility without requiring deterministic replay

The system must preserve sufficient AI execution context while recognizing that identical prompts do not necessarily produce identical outputs.

### Govern cost and resource use

Autonomous or iterative AI operations may consume tokens, compute, API quotas, cloud resources, and financial budget.

### Preserve security and privacy boundaries

Model context must remain subject to the same access, sensitivity, and data-governance constraints as other system operations.

# Detailed architecture

## 1. AI participation model

Monad SHALL model AI participation as a collection of explicit roles rather than a single undifferentiated concept of "AI."

An AI participant MAY act in one or more of the following roles:

```text
assistant
actor
delegate
executor
producer
evaluator
tool
```

The roles MAY coexist during one workflow.

For example, an AI participant may:

1. act as an **assistant** while interpreting a user's request,
2. act as a **producer** while generating a proposed patch,
3. act as a **delegate** when operating under authority granted by a human actor,
4. act as an **executor** when invoking a governed repository capability,
5. and act as an **evaluator** when examining the resulting artifact.

Those roles SHALL NOT be inferred to be equivalent.

In particular:

```text
producer ≠ approver

executor ≠ authority source

evaluator ≠ verifier automatically

assistant ≠ delegate automatically
```

---

## 2. AI identity model

Consequential AI activity SHALL retain sufficient identity to distinguish the participants and executions involved.

The identity model SHOULD distinguish at least:

```text
AI provider
model family
model identifier
model snapshot or revision where available
agent
session
AI execution
actor
human delegator
```

A model name alone is insufficient identity for consequential provenance.

For example:

```text
model = "example-model"
```

does not necessarily identify:

* which provider served the model,
* which model revision was used,
* whether an alias changed,
* which agent configuration invoked it,
* which session supplied context,
* which execution generated an output,
* or under whose delegated authority it acted.

The architecture SHALL therefore avoid treating the marketing or alias name of a model as the complete execution identity.

### Model aliases

Mutable model aliases MAY be recorded for usability but SHALL NOT be treated as immutable model identity.

Where the provider exposes a stronger identifier such as:

* version,
* snapshot,
* revision,
* deployment ID,
* build,
* or equivalent,

Monad SHOULD preserve it.

---

## 3. Observation context

AI reasoning operates over an observation context.

The observation context SHALL retain sufficient provenance to distinguish information according to origin and authority.

At minimum, the architecture SHOULD support provenance categories equivalent to:

```text
system rules
developer instructions
user instructions
canonical artifacts
native systems
derived artifacts
search results
external content
AI inference
persistent memory
```

These categories SHALL NOT be collapsed merely because they appear within the same model context.

An item retrieved from a repository is not a developer instruction merely because the model can read it.

A web page is not system policy.

A generated artifact is not canonical merely because it is present in context.

A remembered statement is not current native state.

An inferred relationship is not an observation.

---

## 4. External content boundary

Content obtained from external or native systems SHALL be treated as **data**, not as trusted system instruction, unless an explicit trusted-channel mechanism establishes otherwise.

This applies to content obtained from:

```text
repositories
issues
pull requests
web pages
emails
documents
logs
provider APIs
comments
generated files
external databases
third-party integrations
```

The system SHALL NOT allow arbitrary content from those sources to redefine:

* authority,
* policy,
* delegation,
* allowed capabilities,
* target selection,
* system instructions,
* governance constraints,
* or approval requirements.

Core invariant:

```text
external content ≠ trusted instruction
```

This boundary is foundational to Monad's prompt-injection resistance.

---

## 5. Reasoning outputs

AI reasoning may produce several semantically different outputs.

Monad SHALL distinguish at least:

```text
Claim
hypothesis
recommendation
plan
proposed action
generated artifact
evaluation
```

These outputs MAY be related but SHALL NOT be treated as interchangeable.

For example:

```text
AI inferred that deployment X is stale
```

is not equivalent to:

```text
native system reported deployment X is stale
```

Similarly:

```text
AI recommends deleting resource X
```

does not imply:

```text
resource X is authorized for deletion
```

and:

```text
AI generated patch Y
```

does not imply:

```text
patch Y is canonical
```

---

## 6. Epistemic state and uncertainty

AI-produced information SHALL preserve epistemic distinction where material.

The architecture SHOULD support states equivalent to:

```text
known
observed
inferred
hypothesized
estimated
recommended
unknown
```

Implementations MAY use more precise vocabularies, but they SHALL NOT collapse meaningful uncertainty into binary truth where the underlying information is probabilistic or incomplete.

Model confidence SHALL NOT itself establish truth.

Confidence values MAY be retained as metadata but SHALL NOT replace Evidence.

---

## 7. AI Claims

An AI-produced Claim SHALL be treated as a Claim, not automatically as authoritative fact.

Important Claims SHOULD resolve, where possible, to one or more of:

```text
source
observation
Evidence
native-system state
explicit human input
derived relationship
```

The system SHOULD preserve the derivation chain between supporting information and the resulting AI Claim.

Where no sufficient grounding exists, the Claim SHOULD remain explicitly inferred, hypothesized, estimated, or otherwise uncertain.

Core invariant:

```text
AI Claim ≠ Fact automatically
```

---

## 8. Native identity and entity resolution

AI SHALL prefer deterministic native-resource identity where native systems expose exact identifiers.

For consequential operations, Monad SHOULD use provider-native identifiers such as:

* repository IDs,
* issue IDs,
* pull-request IDs,
* deployment IDs,
* project IDs,
* resource IDs,
* environment IDs,
* account IDs,
* object URIs,
* or equivalent stable references.

AI SHOULD NOT invent consequential native-resource identity when a deterministic identifier is available.

### Fuzzy resolution

AI MAY use semantic or fuzzy matching to propose an identity relationship.

For example, AI MAY infer that:

```text
"the release checklist"
```

likely refers to a particular document.

That inference MAY become a proposed binding.

It SHALL NOT automatically become an authoritative consequential binding.

A consequential identity mapping SHOULD require sufficient Evidence, deterministic resolution, explicit context, or governed confirmation.

---

## 9. Delegation model

AI authority SHALL originate from an explicit authority model rather than from the AI itself.

Where an AI acts on behalf of another actor, Monad SHALL represent delegation sufficiently to answer:

```text
who delegated?
what was delegated?
under what authority?
for what scope?
for what duration?
which capabilities?
which target systems?
under what constraints?
under which policy?
how may the delegation be revoked?
```

A delegation record SHOULD therefore be capable of representing:

```text
delegator
delegate
authority basis
scope
capabilities
targets
constraints
environment
effective time
expiration
policy references
approval requirements
budget constraints
revocation state
```

### Authority containment

Delegation SHALL NOT create broader authority than the delegator possesses.

Core invariant:

```text
delegated authority ≤ delegator authority
```

An actor without authority to deploy production cannot validly delegate production deployment authority to an AI.

An AI cannot increase its own authority merely because it has access to a capability capable of performing that deployment.

---

## 10. Capability versus authority

Monad SHALL preserve a strict architectural distinction between capability and authority.

A capability represents what the system can technically perform.

Authority represents whether performing that action is legitimately permitted under the governing model.

Therefore:

```text
AI can invoke tool ≠ AI is authorized to perform action
```

and:

```text
Capability ≠ Permission

Credential ≠ Authority
```

A credential may make an API request technically possible while policy still forbids the operation.

The architecture SHALL evaluate consequential AI actions against authority and policy independently of whether the underlying integration accepts the credentials.

---

## 11. Read versus mutation

Read operations and consequential mutations MAY have different governance requirements.

Examples of typically lower-consequence operations include:

```text
read repository
search documentation
inspect issue
read build metadata
retrieve requirement
inspect deployment state
```

Examples of consequential mutation include:

```text
merge pull request
deploy production
delete resource
change policy
approve release
rotate credential
modify infrastructure
publish external communication
change authority
```

Monad SHALL NOT assume that authorization to read a system implies authorization to mutate it.

Likewise:

```text
read access ≠ write authority
```

Implementations SHOULD allow policy to distinguish read, propose, mutate, approve, and verify capabilities.

---

## 12. Consequential action classification

Monad SHALL support policy evaluation of AI operations according to their consequences.

Relevant dimensions MAY include:

```text
reversibility
blast radius
environment
financial impact
security impact
privacy impact
data-loss potential
governance impact
external communication
production mutation
authority change
legal or compliance impact
resource consumption
```

The architecture SHALL NOT prescribe one universal risk-tier vocabulary.

Implementations MAY define operational classes or risk tiers appropriate to their domain.

However, policy SHALL be able to apply stronger controls as consequences increase.

Controls MAY include:

* explicit approval,
* independent approval,
* stronger authentication,
* narrower target binding,
* additional preconditions,
* required Evidence,
* state revalidation,
* dry-run requirements,
* rollback preparation,
* post-action verification,
* separation of duties,
* lower budget limits,
* or prohibition.

---

## 13. Proposal versus execution

The architecture SHALL represent proposal and execution as distinct events.

Monad must be able to distinguish:

```text
AI suggested this change
```

from:

```text
AI performed this change
```

from:

```text
human approved this change
```

A generated plan or proposed action SHALL NOT itself be interpreted as authorization to execute.

Core invariants:

```text
AI recommendation ≠ Decision

AI proposal ≠ Approval

AI proposal ≠ Execution

Plan accepted ≠ Operation authorized automatically
```

---

## 14. Proposed action model

A consequential AI proposal SHOULD identify the intended action sufficiently to support policy evaluation and approval.

A proposed action SHOULD be capable of binding:

```text
operation
capability
target
environment
input or revision
expected state
intended effect
constraints
required approvals
policy context
```

Where the operation depends upon a particular resource revision, configuration state, or environment, those values SHOULD be represented as preconditions rather than remaining implicit in prose.

---

## 15. Human approval

Human approval SHALL be required when governing policy requires it.

Approval SHALL bind to a sufficiently precise consequential operation.

Generic approvals such as:

```text
approved AI
```

or:

```text
AI may do whatever is necessary
```

SHOULD NOT be treated as sufficient authorization for a consequential mutation unless a legitimate policy explicitly defines such broad delegated scope.

Preferred approval semantics bind:

```text
approved operation
approved target
approved revision
approved environment
approved scope
approved constraints
```

Approval MAY authorize:

* one operation,
* a bounded operation set,
* a bounded time interval,
* a specific work packet,
* a specific environment,
* or a predefined autonomous policy scope.

Approval SHALL remain distinct from authority itself.

---

## 16. Autonomous execution

Monad SHALL support autonomous AI execution where governance explicitly permits it.

Per-operation human approval is therefore not universally required.

Instead, autonomy SHALL be expressed through bounded delegated authority and policy.

An autonomous execution scope MAY define:

```text
allowed capabilities
allowed targets
allowed environments
maximum operations
maximum duration
maximum cost
maximum resource consumption
required preconditions
stop conditions
escalation conditions
prohibited actions
verification requirements
```

An AI acting within that scope MAY execute without additional approval where policy permits.

An operation outside the scope SHALL require additional authority or SHALL be denied.

---

## 17. Target resolution

Consequential target resolution SHALL be explicit.

AI SHALL NOT guess a consequential target when multiple plausible targets exist.

Examples include:

```text
production account
staging account

repository A
repository B

cloud project X
cloud project Y

branch main
branch release

database primary
database replica
```

If target resolution remains ambiguous, the action SHALL NOT proceed as though one candidate were known to be correct.

The system SHOULD produce an explicit ambiguous-target outcome.

Core invariant:

```text
AI-selected target ≠ Valid target automatically
```

---

## 18. Production targeting

Production SHALL NOT become an inferred default.

An AI SHALL NOT choose production merely because:

* a name appears primary,
* no other target was specified,
* the environment looks most active,
* a repository is the default repository,
* an account name resembles production,
* or historical context suggests production was used previously.

Core invariant:

```text
Production ≠ Default target
```

Production access SHALL be established explicitly through target resolution and policy.

---

## 19. Preconditions

Consequential AI operations SHOULD support explicit preconditions.

Relevant preconditions include:

```text
expected revision
expected current state
required approval
required Evidence
required environment
expected policy revision
expected resource identity
expected authority state
expected dependency state
```

Preconditions allow an operation to fail safely when reality no longer matches the assumptions under which the AI planned the action.

---

## 20. Plan staleness

Planning and execution SHALL be modeled as distinct points in time.

An AI-generated plan may become stale because:

* source code changed,
* policy changed,
* approval expired,
* deployment state changed,
* another actor modified the target,
* a native resource was replaced,
* environment configuration changed,
* Evidence became outdated,
* or an external dependency changed.

Therefore:

```text
Plan ≠ Current state
```

The existence of a valid plan at time `T1` SHALL NOT imply that the plan remains safe to execute at time `T2`.

---

## 21. Pre-execution revalidation

Immediately before a consequential mutation, Monad SHOULD revalidate material assumptions where feasible.

Revalidation MAY include:

```text
target identity
current revision
current state
approval validity
delegation validity
policy revision
capability availability
environment
resource existence
conflicting mutation
required Evidence
budget availability
```

If a material precondition no longer holds, the operation SHOULD be halted, re-planned, or escalated according to policy.

---

## 22. Tool invocation model

Consequential AI tool invocation SHALL preserve sufficient provenance to reconstruct what occurred.

An invocation record SHOULD retain:

```text
AI execution ID
AI participant identity
capability
arguments
resolved target
integration
actor
delegation
authority basis
policy decision
approval reference
timestamps
native request
native response
execution result
Evidence
```

Sensitive values MAY be referenced rather than stored directly.

The invocation record SHALL distinguish the requested operation from the observed native effect.

---

## 23. Credentials and secrets

Credentials SHALL be treated as implementation mechanisms for capabilities, not as authority.

The possession of credentials SHALL NOT establish legitimacy to use every capability those credentials technically expose.

Secrets SHOULD NOT be inserted into AI prompts or general model context unless strictly required and explicitly permitted.

Where possible, tool execution SHOULD use opaque secret references or platform-managed credentials.

The AI may therefore invoke:

```text
credential-reference: deploy-prod
```

without receiving the underlying secret value.

---

## 24. Execution result semantics

The absence of a tool error SHALL NOT establish that the intended semantic effect occurred.

Core invariant:

```text
tool call returned successfully ≠ desired state achieved
```

Additional invariants include:

```text
API success ≠ Semantic success

Mutation accepted ≠ Mutation completed

Completed ≠ Verified

No error ≠ Success
```

For example, a deployment API may return an accepted response while the deployment later fails.

A pull-request merge request may succeed while a subsequent automation reverts the change.

A file-write command may return successfully while the resulting content does not satisfy the intended requirement.

Execution result and semantic outcome SHALL therefore remain distinct.

---

## 25. Unknown outcomes

Monad SHALL represent unknown or ambiguous execution outcomes explicitly.

A provider timeout occurring after a mutation request is a canonical example.

The system may not know whether:

```text
request failed before mutation
```

or:

```text
mutation occurred but response was lost
```

The system SHALL NOT automatically classify this state as failure.

Core invariant:

```text
Unknown outcome ≠ Failed outcome
```

Where repeating the request could create duplicate or destructive effects, the AI SHOULD observe current native state before retrying.

Core invariant:

```text
Retryable ≠ Safe to repeat blindly
```

---

## 26. Error taxonomy

AI execution SHOULD distinguish operational failure classes rather than collapsing them into an undifferentiated error.

The architecture SHOULD support outcomes equivalent to:

```text
tool unavailable
authentication failure
authorization denial
resource missing
resource ambiguous
provider timeout
unknown outcome
partial success
precondition failure
policy denial
budget exceeded
verification failure
```

This distinction is important because the correct recovery action differs substantially between classes.

For example:

* authentication failure may require credential repair,
* authorization denial may require governance escalation,
* resource ambiguity requires target resolution,
* unknown outcome requires observation,
* precondition failure requires replanning,
* verification failure requires investigation rather than blind re-execution.

---

## 27. Post-action observation

Consequential mutations SHOULD be followed by native observation where the intended effect cannot be established reliably from the initial tool response.

Post-action observation MAY determine:

```text
whether the resource changed
what native identifier was created
what final state exists
whether asynchronous work completed
whether side effects occurred
whether the expected revision is active
```

Observation SHALL remain distinct from Verification.

---

## 28. Evidence and verification

AI execution MAY produce Evidence.

AI output itself SHALL NOT automatically constitute independent Evidence of the state it claims to have produced.

Core invariant:

```text
Model output ≠ Evidence automatically
```

Similarly:

```text
AI-generated Evidence ≠ Independent Evidence automatically
```

Where an AI reports:

```text
deployment succeeded
```

the system SHOULD prefer Evidence derived from the deployment system, resulting environment, verification system, or another appropriately governed source.

---

## 29. AI evaluation

AI MAY evaluate artifacts and system state.

Examples include:

* code review,
* specification comparison,
* policy analysis,
* architecture consistency analysis,
* test interpretation,
* documentation review,
* anomaly detection.

Such evaluation SHALL NOT automatically be classified as Verification.

Core invariant:

```text
AI evaluation ≠ Verification automatically
```

An AI evaluator MAY participate in Verification only under an explicit verification contract.

---

## 30. AI verification contract

Where AI participates in formal Verification, the verification contract SHOULD define at least:

```text
model identity
method
inputs
criteria
uncertainty handling
required corroboration
acceptable failure modes
required Evidence
```

The contract MAY require:

* independent native Evidence,
* deterministic checks,
* multiple evaluators,
* human review,
* comparison against a specification,
* structured scoring,
* or explicit uncertainty thresholds.

A probabilistic judgment becomes a Verification result only when the governing verification architecture recognizes that method as valid for the specific verification obligation.

---

## 31. Separation of duties

Monad policy MAY require distinct participants for:

```text
proposal
approval
execution
verification
```

AI participation SHALL NOT erase these boundaries.

For example, policy may prohibit the same AI execution from:

```text
generating a privileged policy change
approving the policy change
executing the change
and independently verifying the change
```

Where separation of duties is required, identity and provenance SHALL make violations detectable.

Core invariant:

```text
AI approval of own action ≠ Independent approval
```

---

## 32. Self-modification

AI SHALL NOT casually rewrite the governance structures that constrain its own operation.

Protected domains include:

```text
system constitution
authority rules
policy
delegation
capability boundaries
approval rules
verification rules
```

Changes to these artifacts MAY be proposed by AI.

They MAY even be executed by AI where governance permits.

However, such changes SHALL follow the same or stronger governance that applies to equivalent changes performed by other actors.

An AI SHALL NOT gain authority merely because it can edit the representation of authority.

Core invariants:

```text
Policy edit ≠ Authority acquisition

Ability to modify policy ≠ Permission to modify policy
```

---

## 33. Circular authority escalation

Monad SHALL prevent circular AI authority escalation.

The following sequence is an architectural anti-pattern:

```text
AI edits policy granting itself permission
AI approves the policy change
AI treats the resulting permission as legitimate
AI executes privileged operation
```

Authority evaluation SHALL resolve through legitimate governance rather than merely through the current contents of a file the AI is technically capable of modifying.

---

## 34. Recursive delegation

An AI delegate SHALL NOT freely delegate its authority to additional AI participants unless the governing policy explicitly permits recursive delegation.

Where recursive delegation is allowed, the delegated scope SHALL remain bounded by:

```text
parent delegation
policy
capability restrictions
target restrictions
duration
budget
environment
```

Recursive delegation SHALL NOT expand authority beyond the valid parent delegation chain.

---

## 35. Multi-agent systems

In a multi-agent system, each consequential participant SHALL preserve its own:

```text
identity
role
delegated scope
execution provenance
inputs
outputs
capability invocations
```

Multi-agent consensus SHALL NOT automatically establish truth.

Core invariant:

```text
Multi-agent consensus ≠ Truth
```

Multiple agents producing the same inference may increase confidence according to a defined method, but agreement does not replace Evidence or Verification.

---

## 36. AI-generated artifacts

Consequential artifacts generated by AI SHOULD retain provenance.

Examples include:

```text
source code
documentation
migrations
policy
specifications
configuration
infrastructure definitions
test cases
release artifacts
generated commands
```

Provenance SHOULD permit later determination of:

* which AI execution generated the artifact,
* which model participated,
* which input context was used,
* which source artifacts were referenced,
* which human or automated actor requested the generation,
* and what subsequent transformations occurred.

Generated artifacts SHALL NOT become canonical solely because they were generated.

Core invariant:

```text
Generated ≠ Canonical automatically
```

---

## 37. Human edits after generation

Human modification of AI-generated content SHALL create subsequent provenance rather than erasing the AI-generation history.

A provenance chain may therefore resemble:

```text
AI generation
      │
      ▼
human modification
      │
      ▼
review
      │
      ▼
approval
      │
      ▼
canonicalization
```

The final artifact may become authoritative through normal governance while retaining its derivation history.

---

## 38. Persistent AI memory

Persistent AI memory MAY assist future reasoning.

Memory SHALL NOT automatically become authoritative engineering truth.

Core invariant:

```text
Memory ≠ Authority
```

A remembered statement SHOULD retain provenance and freshness information when used consequentially.

Where current native state matters, memory SHOULD be revalidated against an authoritative or appropriately current source.

---

## 39. Context freshness

Retrieved context SHALL NOT be silently treated as current native state when freshness is unknown or stale.

Core invariant:

```text
Retrieved context ≠ Current truth automatically
```

Relevant context SHOULD retain, where available:

```text
observation time
source revision
native revision
retrieval time
freshness status
```

When consequential execution depends on current state, stale context SHOULD trigger re-observation.

---

## 40. Model drift

AI model behavior may change over time.

Mutable aliases, provider-side updates, inference infrastructure, safety layers, and serving configuration can affect results.

Consequential AI provenance SHOULD therefore preserve the strongest available model identity.

When exact model revision cannot be obtained, Monad SHOULD record the strongest available information without pretending that stronger reproducibility exists.

---

## 41. AI reproducibility

AI execution SHALL integrate with the reproducibility architecture established by ADR-0009.

Where available and permissible, consequential AI execution SHOULD preserve:

```text
provider
model identifier
model snapshot
agent identity
system instructions
developer instructions
user request
prompt
context provenance
generation parameters
tool calls
tool arguments
outputs
timestamps
environment
policy revision
delegation
approval references
```

Sensitive data MAY be redacted, encrypted, represented by digest, or referenced indirectly according to policy.

Exact deterministic replay SHALL NOT be universally required.

Core invariants:

```text
Same prompt ≠ Same output

AI reproducibility ≠ Deterministic replay
```

The objective is sufficient reconstruction and explanation, not an impossible guarantee that every probabilistic generation can be reproduced byte-for-byte.

---

## 42. Sensitive data boundaries

AI context SHALL obey data-access, privacy, classification, and provider-boundary policy.

Technical access to a data source SHALL NOT imply permission to include all available data in model context.

The system SHOULD minimize contextual disclosure according to the task and authority scope.

Examples of governed decisions include:

```text
whether data may leave the local machine
whether data may be sent to a hosted model
whether secrets may enter context
whether personal information may be transmitted
whether proprietary source may be shared with provider
whether retention is permitted
```

---

## 43. AI provider trust boundary

Sending information to an external AI provider SHALL be treated as an external-system operation.

Provider access SHOULD therefore be governed by:

* data policy,
* classification,
* privacy requirements,
* contractual constraints,
* security policy,
* retention constraints,
* and integration configuration.

The existence of an AI provider integration SHALL NOT itself authorize transmission of every artifact available to Monad.

---

## 44. Cost and resource governance

AI activity MAY consume governed resources including:

```text
tokens
API cost
compute
cloud resources
storage
external API quota
wall-clock time
human review capacity
```

Delegation and policy MAY therefore specify resource budgets.

Autonomous AI execution SHOULD support limits such as:

```text
maximum operations
maximum model cost
maximum compute
maximum duration
maximum retries
maximum external requests
```

Budget exhaustion SHOULD be represented explicitly rather than treated as an arbitrary tool failure.

---

## 45. Autonomous loops

Autonomous AI loops SHALL have bounded behavior.

A loop SHOULD support constraints equivalent to:

```text
maximum operations
maximum cost
maximum duration
allowed capabilities
allowed targets
allowed environments
stop conditions
escalation conditions
```

The system SHOULD prevent unbounded recursive behavior where no explicit policy has granted it.

A loop SHOULD terminate, pause, or escalate when:

* authority is insufficient,
* the target becomes ambiguous,
* preconditions fail,
* verification repeatedly fails,
* budget is exhausted,
* execution outcome becomes unknown,
* policy changes,
* or the stop condition is reached.

---

## 46. Auditability

Consequential AI activity SHALL be reconstructable to a degree appropriate to its impact.

The architecture SHOULD permit an auditor, engineer, reviewer, or future AI to answer:

```text
What did the AI know?

What information was observed?

What information was inferred?

What did the AI propose?

Which model produced the reasoning?

What was the model identity?

What authority was delegated?

Who delegated it?

What policy applied?

What capability did the AI invoke?

What arguments were supplied?

What target was resolved?

What preconditions were checked?

What native request occurred?

What native effect occurred?

What Evidence was collected?

What verification followed?

What remained uncertain?
```

No single log entry is required to contain all of this information.

The architecture SHALL, however, preserve sufficient relationships among the relevant artifacts to reconstruct the chain.

---

## 47. Reference interaction model

A consequential AI interaction SHOULD be representable conceptually as:

```text
Human / Governing Actor
        │
        │ delegates bounded authority
        ▼
Delegation
        │
        ▼
AI Participant
        │
        ├── observes context
        │
        ├── reasons
        │
        ├── produces Claims
        │
        ├── generates proposal
        │
        └── requests operation
        │
        ▼
Proposed Action
        │
        ▼
Authority / Policy Evaluation
        │
        ├── deny
        │
        ├── require approval
        │
        └── authorize
        │
        ▼
Governed Capability
        │
        ▼
Integration
        │
        ▼
Native System
        │
        ▼
Observed Effect
        │
        ▼
Evidence
        │
        ▼
Verification / Reconciliation
```

Each transition MAY create or reference provenance.

# Alternatives considered

## Alternative 1 — Treat AI as an ordinary tool only

Under this model, AI would be represented no differently from a compiler, text editor, or shell utility.

### Rejected because

This hides delegation, reasoning provenance, uncertainty, proposal semantics, context provenance, autonomous operation, and model identity.

AI can act as a tool in some workflows, but the architecture cannot assume that tool semantics fully describe AI participation.

---

## Alternative 2 — Treat AI as inherently authoritative

Under this model, an AI selected by the system would be trusted to make and execute engineering decisions by virtue of being the designated AI.

### Rejected because

Model competence does not establish governance legitimacy.

Probabilistic reasoning cannot silently become authority.

This model would collapse:

```text
recommendation
decision
approval
authority
execution
verification
```

into one actor and violate the existing Monad authority architecture.

---

## Alternative 3 — Treat possession of credentials as permission

Under this model, any operation technically possible using the AI's available credentials would be permitted.

### Rejected because

Credentials implement access.

They do not establish authority.

This model would make provider configuration the de facto governance system and would bypass Monad's authority and policy architecture.

---

## Alternative 4 — Require human approval for every AI mutation

Under this model, all AI writes would require an explicit human confirmation immediately before execution.

### Rejected because

It prevents legitimate bounded autonomy and does not itself solve target ambiguity, stale state, weak provenance, prompt injection, or inadequate verification.

Human approval remains available as a policy mechanism but is not the universal architectural definition of AI safety.

---

## Alternative 5 — Treat an approved plan as authority for arbitrary later execution

Under this model, approval of an AI-generated plan would implicitly authorize any later actions interpreted as implementing that plan.

### Rejected because

Plans may be ambiguous or stale.

Execution may require different capabilities, targets, revisions, or environments than originally anticipated.

Approval must bind sufficiently to the consequential scope being authorized.

---

## Alternative 6 — Trust external content placed in model context

Under this model, repository documents, web pages, issues, emails, and other retrieved text could alter model behavior as though they were trusted instructions.

### Rejected because

This collapses data and governance instruction and creates a direct prompt-injection path.

---

## Alternative 7 — Treat successful tool invocation as successful engineering outcome

Under this model, a successful API or command response would be sufficient proof that the intended result occurred.

### Rejected because

Native systems frequently expose asynchronous, partial, accepted-but-not-completed, or semantically incomplete outcomes.

Observation and Verification remain necessary.

---

## Alternative 8 — Treat AI evaluation as formal Verification by default

Under this model, an AI reviewing the result of an AI action could declare the result verified.

### Rejected because

Evaluation method, Evidence, independence, uncertainty, and verification criteria must be explicitly defined.

AI may participate in Verification, but only under a verification contract.

---

## Alternative 9 — Require deterministic replay of consequential AI output

Under this model, a consequential AI interaction would be considered reproducible only when rerunning the same prompt produces the same output.

### Rejected because

Many AI systems are probabilistic and provider-controlled.

This would make meaningful reproducibility impossible for legitimate workflows.

Monad instead requires reconstruction of the consequential execution context and preservation of the strongest available model and context provenance.

---

# Consequences

## Positive consequences

### AI can participate broadly

The architecture does not artificially restrict AI to passive recommendation.

AI can inspect, propose, generate, execute, evaluate, and participate in verification when policy permits.

### Autonomy becomes governable

Autonomous operation is represented as bounded delegated authority rather than as an exception to governance.

### Prompt injection receives an architectural boundary

External content remains data rather than becoming policy simply because it enters model context.

### Authority remains explicit

Tool availability and credentials cannot silently replace the authority model.

### AI operations become auditable

Consequential actions can be reconstructed across reasoning, delegation, execution, Evidence, and verification.

### Native-system truth remains authoritative where appropriate

AI observations and inferences remain distinct from provider-native state.

### Stale planning can be detected

Preconditions and revalidation provide an explicit defense against plans becoming invalid before execution.

### Multi-agent systems remain traceable

Each participant retains identity, scope, and provenance.

### Reproducibility becomes realistic

The architecture captures model and context provenance without requiring impossible deterministic replay.

---

## Negative consequences

### More metadata

Consequential AI workflows require additional provenance, delegation, policy, and execution metadata.

### More explicit modeling

Implementations must distinguish concepts that simplistic agent systems frequently collapse.

### Additional policy infrastructure

Useful autonomy requires authority and policy evaluation capable of expressing bounded delegation.

### Integration complexity

Native integrations must expose enough identity, state, and execution result detail to support reliable target resolution and post-action observation.

### Some actions must stop on ambiguity

The system will occasionally refuse to infer a convenient target even when the AI is highly confident.

### Historical reconstruction may remain incomplete

External AI providers may not expose immutable model revisions or enough serving metadata for exact reconstruction.

Monad must preserve the strongest available information without overstating certainty.

---

# Operational implications

Implementations conforming to this ADR SHOULD provide architectural support for:

* AI execution identifiers,
* model and provider identity,
* agent and session identity,
* delegation records,
* capability descriptions,
* authority evaluation,
* policy decisions,
* target resolution,
* preconditions,
* approval references,
* native invocation provenance,
* execution outcome classification,
* post-action observation,
* Evidence links,
* verification relationships,
* context provenance,
* context freshness,
* autonomous-loop budgets,
* and escalation conditions.

The architecture SHOULD make these mechanisms reusable across integrations rather than embedding AI governance independently inside each provider adapter.

Native integrations SHOULD expose deterministic resource identifiers wherever available.

Policy SHOULD be evaluated independently from whether a tool or provider accepts the underlying request.

Consequential autonomous execution SHOULD fail closed where:

* target identity is materially ambiguous,
* authority cannot be established,
* required approval is absent,
* required preconditions cannot be validated,
* policy denies the operation,
* or a protected governance boundary would be bypassed.

Unknown execution outcome SHOULD NOT automatically fail closed by retrying. Where duplicate side effects are possible, observation precedes repetition.

# Definition of Ready

Implementation work governed by ADR-0010 is ready when:

* [ ] the affected AI interaction has an identifiable participant and execution,
* [ ] the relevant capability is known,
* [ ] the authority source can be identified,
* [ ] delegation semantics are defined where AI acts for another actor,
* [ ] consequential targets can be represented deterministically,
* [ ] applicable policy can be evaluated,
* [ ] required approvals can be represented,
* [ ] native execution results can be distinguished from observed effects,
* [ ] Evidence and Verification relationships are available where required,
* [ ] context provenance can distinguish instruction from retrieved data,
* [ ] error states can represent ambiguity and unknown outcomes,
* [ ] reproducibility requirements from ADR-0009 have been considered,
* [ ] sensitive-data boundaries are defined for any external AI provider.

# Definition of Done

An implementation governed by ADR-0010 is complete when:

* [ ] AI identity is retained for consequential activity,
* [ ] model identity uses the strongest available provider identifier,
* [ ] delegation does not exceed delegator authority,
* [ ] capability availability is not used as an authority decision,
* [ ] consequential actions are policy evaluated,
* [ ] proposal, approval, execution, and Verification remain distinct,
* [ ] consequential targets are explicitly resolved,
* [ ] production is never an inferred default,
* [ ] material preconditions are revalidated before mutation where required,
* [ ] external content cannot silently redefine trusted policy or instructions,
* [ ] consequential tool invocations retain execution provenance,
* [ ] unknown outcomes are distinguished from confirmed failures,
* [ ] duplicate-risk retries observe native state before repetition,
* [ ] consequential mutations support post-action observation where required,
* [ ] AI evaluation is not automatically treated as Verification,
* [ ] generated artifacts retain provenance where consequential,
* [ ] persistent memory is not automatically authoritative,
* [ ] autonomous loops are bounded,
* [ ] recursive delegation obeys policy,
* [ ] sensitive information obeys data-governance boundaries,
* [ ] the resulting activity can be reconstructed sufficiently for review,
* [ ] conformance tests demonstrate the core invariants of this ADR.

# Acceptance criteria

## AC-AI-001 — AI identity is preserved

Given a consequential AI operation, the resulting provenance identifies the AI execution and preserves the strongest available model identity.

---

## AC-AI-002 — Model alias is not sufficient identity

Given a provider exposes both a mutable model alias and a stronger immutable or versioned identifier, consequential provenance preserves the stronger identifier.

---

## AC-AI-003 — Capability does not establish authority

Given an AI can technically invoke a privileged tool but lacks authority under policy, the invocation is denied or withheld.

---

## AC-AI-004 — Credentials do not establish authority

Given credentials technically permit an operation but governance does not authorize it, the operation does not proceed.

---

## AC-AI-005 — Delegation is bounded

Given a delegator lacks authority for an operation, the system does not allow that actor to confer the missing authority upon an AI.

---

## AC-AI-006 — Proposal remains distinct from execution

Given an AI generates a proposed action, the system can determine that the proposal exists without implying that the action occurred.

---

## AC-AI-007 — Approval is action-bound

Given policy requires approval for a consequential operation, the approval can be associated with the relevant operation, target, scope, revision, or equivalent binding.

---

## AC-AI-008 — Autonomous action requires explicit authority

Given no per-operation human approval is present, an AI operation proceeds only when policy and delegation explicitly authorize execution within the applicable scope.

---

## AC-AI-009 — Ambiguous targets halt consequential execution

Given multiple plausible consequential targets remain unresolved, the AI does not silently choose one and execute.

---

## AC-AI-010 — Production is not inferred

Given no environment was legitimately resolved, the AI does not select production merely as a default.

---

## AC-AI-011 — External content cannot redefine governance

Given retrieved content contains instructions attempting to grant authority, alter policy, or redirect protected capabilities, those instructions remain untrusted data unless validated through an authorized instruction channel.

---

## AC-AI-012 — Inference remains distinguishable from observation

Given an AI infers a system condition from incomplete context, the resulting information is not recorded as though directly observed from the native system.

---

## AC-AI-013 — Stale plans are revalidated

Given material target state changes between plan creation and execution, required precondition validation detects the mismatch before consequential mutation.

---

## AC-AI-014 — Tool success is not semantic success

Given a provider accepts an operation but the intended state is not subsequently achieved, the architecture can represent that distinction.

---

## AC-AI-015 — Unknown outcome is preserved

Given a provider timeout occurs after a potentially non-idempotent mutation request, the result can be represented as unknown rather than automatically failed.

---

## AC-AI-016 — Blind retry is prevented

Given an operation has an unknown outcome and duplicate effects are possible, the system observes native state before retrying.

---

## AC-AI-017 — Post-action Evidence can be attached

Given a consequential mutation completes, resulting native observations or other Evidence can be associated with that execution.

---

## AC-AI-018 — AI evaluation is not automatically Verification

Given an AI evaluates an artifact, the evaluation does not become formal Verification unless an applicable verification contract authorizes the method.

---

## AC-AI-019 — Generated provenance survives human modification

Given AI generates an artifact and a human later modifies it, provenance retains both stages rather than replacing the generation history.

---

## AC-AI-020 — Memory is not treated as current truth

Given persistent memory conflicts with current authoritative native state, the architecture permits the current observation to supersede the remembered information without treating memory as authority.

---

## AC-AI-021 — Autonomous loops are bounded

Given an autonomous AI workflow exceeds an applicable operation, cost, duration, or other configured limit, execution stops or escalates according to policy.

---

## AC-AI-022 — Self-authorization is rejected

Given an AI modifies policy in an attempt to grant itself additional authority without legitimate governance, the resulting policy change cannot bootstrap valid authority for the privileged operation.

---

## AC-AI-023 — Multi-agent identity is preserved

Given multiple agents participate in a consequential workflow, the system can distinguish their identities, roles, delegated scopes, and executions.

---

## AC-AI-024 — Sensitive data remains governed

Given an AI provider is connected, data outside the permitted disclosure scope is not automatically included in model context merely because the AI can technically retrieve it.

---

## AC-AI-025 — Consequential activity is reconstructable

Given a completed consequential AI action, a reviewer can determine the material observation context, proposal, authority basis, capability invocation, target, native effect, Evidence, and verification state.

---


---

```text

```

without a legitimate governed scope.

## Stale-plan execution


# Governing principle

> **Monad should allow AI to reason broadly and act usefully while ensuring that probabilistic inference, technical capability, credential possession, and generated output never silently become authority.**

More operationally:

> **AI may participate throughout the engineering system, but consequential action must remain bounded by explicit identity, authority, policy, provenance, target resolution, and verification.**

## Final architectural statement

Monad does not make AI safe by pretending AI cannot act.

Monad makes consequential AI action governable by refusing to collapse reasoning, authority, capability, execution, Evidence, and verification into one opaque agent operation.

The system SHALL permit AI to inspect broadly, reason probabilistically, propose useful work, and execute meaningful engineering operations when legitimately authorized.

At every consequential boundary, however, Monad SHALL retain enough structure to determine:

```text
who or what participated,
what was known,
what was inferred,
what was proposed,
what authority existed,
what policy applied,
what target was resolved,
what capability was invoked,
what native effect occurred,
what Evidence resulted,
and what was actually verified.
```

That separation is the architectural basis for useful AI autonomy without silent transfer of authority.
