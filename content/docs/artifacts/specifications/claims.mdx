# Claims Specification

**Specification ID:** `SPEC-CLM-001`
**Status:** Draft
**Version:** `0.1.0`
**Normative:** Yes
**Primary requirements:** `REQ-SEM-*`
**Related requirements:** `REQ-EVD-*`, `REQ-PROV-*`, `REQ-AUTH-*`, `REQ-GOV-*`, `REQ-REC-*`
**Parent specification:** `SPEC-SEM-001 — Semantic Kernel Specification`

## 1. Purpose

This specification defines how Monad represents assertions about an engineering system.

A `Claim` is a first-class semantic assertion that expresses what some actor, source, process, artifact, observation, inference, or system says about some subject.

Claims allow Monad to represent engineering knowledge without prematurely collapsing:

```text
what was asserted
        ↓
what evidence exists
        ↓
who had authority
        ↓
what Monad currently accepts
        ↓
what is actually true
```

into a single field.

The central distinction is:

> **A Claim is an assertion, not truth itself.**

A Claim may be:

* correct,
* incorrect,
* supported,
* contradicted,
* unresolved,
* inferred,
* authoritative,
* unauthorized,
* obsolete,
* superseded,
* historically true,
* currently false,
* impossible to verify.

The Claim remains useful because it records that the assertion existed and allows Monad to reason explicitly about its epistemic status.

# 4. Core Claim Model

Conceptually:

```text
Claim
 ├── identifies a proposition
 ├── concerns one or more Subjects
 ├── has provenance
 ├── may have Evidence
 ├── may have authority
 ├── may have confidence
 ├── may conflict with other Claims
 ├── may have temporal scope
 └── may have lifecycle state
```

A minimal Claim may be understood as:

```text
Claim {
    identity
    proposition
    provenance
}
```

A richer Claim may include:

```text
Claim {
    identity
    proposition
    subject
    predicate
    value
    claimType
    scope
    validTime
    observedTime
    producer
    provenance
    confidence
    authority
    lifecycle
}
```

The final serialized representation is deferred.

---

# 5. Core Claim Invariants

Conforming implementations MUST preserve the following distinctions.

## 5.1 Claim ≠ truth

The existence of a Claim does not establish that its proposition is true.

```text
Claim exists
    ≠
Claim is true
```

---

## 5.2 Claim ≠ Evidence

A Claim states something.

Evidence supports, contradicts, contextualizes, or otherwise bears on that assertion.

```text
Claim
    ≠
Evidence
```

---

## 5.3 Claim ≠ authority

A Claim may originate from an authoritative source.

That does not make Claim and authority the same concept.

```text
Claim
    ≠
Authority
```

---

## 5.4 Claim ≠ provenance

Provenance explains where a Claim came from.

It does not constitute the proposition asserted.

---

## 5.5 Claim ≠ verification

A verification result may generate Claims.

The Claim itself is not the verification procedure.

---

## 5.6 Confidence ≠ truth

A highly confident Claim may be false.

A low-confidence Claim may ultimately be correct.

---

## 5.7 Confidence ≠ authority

An actor may be highly confident while lacking authority.

An authoritative source may issue a Claim with uncertainty.

---

## 5.8 Contradiction ≠ automatic resolution

Two contradictory Claims MAY coexist.

Monad MUST NOT erase one merely because another contradicts it.

---

## 5.9 Unknown ≠ false

Absence of a supported Claim does not necessarily establish the negation of that Claim.

---

## 5.10 Historical truth ≠ current truth

A Claim may have been valid in the past and no longer be valid now.

---

# 6. Claim as a First-Class Entity

Claims that materially participate in engineering reasoning SHOULD be first-class semantic entities.

First-class treatment enables Claims to have:

* stable identity,
* provenance,
* relationships,
* lifecycle,
* Evidence,
* authority,
* temporal semantics,
* revision history.

Monad MUST NOT require every incidental sentence in every document to become a first-class Claim.

Formalization SHOULD be proportional to engineering value.

---

# 7. Claim Identity

A persisted first-class Claim MUST possess stable identity.

Claim identity MUST remain distinct from:

* textual wording,
* source location,
* source artifact identity,
* Subject identity,
* Evidence identity,
* producer identity.

The same proposition MAY be represented by more than one Claim when provenance, temporal scope, authority, or epistemic status materially differs.

---

# 8. Claim Identity and Wording

Changing the wording of a Claim does not necessarily create a new Claim.

For example:

```text
"Component A depends on Component B."
```

and:

```text
"A has a dependency on B."
```

may express the same proposition.

Whether they are the same Claim depends on:

* Claim provenance,
* identity continuity,
* revision semantics,
* whether the proposition materially changed.

String equality MUST NOT define Claim identity.

---

# 9. Claim Identity and Proposition

A Claim and its proposition MUST remain conceptually distinguishable.

A proposition represents what is being asserted.

A Claim represents an assertion of that proposition in a particular semantic and provenance context.

Conceptually:

```text
Proposition P:
    Service A depends on Library B.

Claim C1:
    Architecture document asserts P.

Claim C2:
    Dependency scanner asserts P.

Claim C3:
    AI analysis infers P.
```

These Claims MAY concern the same proposition while remaining distinct Claims.

---

# 10. Proposition Representation

Monad MUST support a Claim representation capable of preserving the proposition being asserted.

A proposition MAY initially be represented as:

* opaque prose,
* structured predicate/value form,
* typed structured expression,
* another representation defined by a domain specification.

The semantic model MUST NOT require all Claims to reach the same formalization level.

---

# 11. Progressive Formalization

Claims MAY progress from less structured to more structured forms.

For example:

```text
Level 0
"Authentication depends on the identity service."
```

```text
Level 1
subject: Authentication
predicate: depends-on
object: Identity Service
```

```text
Level 2
subject: subject:auth
predicate: monad.relationship.depends-on
object: subject:identity-service
scope: runtime
```

Higher formalization MUST be introduced only when it provides sufficient engineering value.

---

# 12. Opaque Claims

Monad MAY preserve Claims whose proposition remains opaque prose.

For example:

```text
"The current deployment process is too fragile."
```

An opaque Claim MAY still carry:

* identity,
* provenance,
* producer,
* time,
* Evidence,
* confidence,
* authority,
* lifecycle.

Opaque Claims MUST NOT be treated as semantically meaningless merely because they are not machine-normalized.

---

# 13. Structured Claims

Where machine reasoning materially benefits, a Claim SHOULD support structured proposition representation.

A common conceptual form is:

```text
subject
predicate
object-or-value
```

For example:

```text
subject: Payment Service
predicate: deployed-to
object: Production
```

or:

```text
subject: Build Pipeline
predicate: duration
value: 12m 42s
```

This specification does not mandate RDF or any particular triple representation.

---

# 14. Typed Values

Structured Claims SHOULD preserve value types where those types affect interpretation.

Examples:

```text
boolean
integer
decimal
string
timestamp
duration
identifier
version
URI
enumeration
structured object
Subject reference
```

The string `"42"` MUST NOT automatically be assumed equivalent to numeric `42`.

---

# 15. Subject of a Claim

A Claim SHOULD identify the semantic entity or entities to which it applies when such identity is known.

For example:

```text
Claim:
    Subject S has lifecycle state "accepted"
```

The Subject SHOULD be referenced using semantic identity rather than mutable representation location where durable reference is required.

---

# 16. Multi-Subject Claims

A Claim MAY concern multiple Subjects.

Examples:

```text
A depends on B.
```

```text
Release R contains Components A, B, and C.
```

```text
Decision D resolves conflict between Requirements X and Y.
```

The Claim model MUST be capable of representing such propositions without artificially assigning all semantics to one Subject.

---

# 17. Claim Predicate

Structured Claims SHOULD use predicates whose semantics are defined sufficiently for reliable interpretation.

A predicate SHOULD specify, directly or through a vocabulary:

* meaning,
* expected subject type,
* expected value/object type,
* cardinality where relevant,
* temporal behavior where relevant.

Monad MUST NOT assume predicates are interchangeable merely because their labels are similar.

---

# 18. Predicate Identity

Predicates requiring stable machine interpretation SHOULD possess stable semantic identity.

For example:

```text
depends-on
```

must remain distinguishable from:

```text
caused-by
```

```text
implemented-by
```

```text
supersedes
```

even if human prose sometimes uses those terms loosely.

---

# 19. Claim Type

A Claim MAY be classified according to its semantic role.

Possible classes include:

```text
observation
assertion
inference
hypothesis
prediction
measurement
interpretation
derived claim
normative claim
verification result
status claim
```

The final taxonomy MUST be defined through the Schema Catalog or domain specifications.

Claim type MUST NOT substitute for provenance.

---

# 20. Observation

An observation Claim reports state perceived from some source.

Example:

```text
"GitHub reports issue #42 as closed."
```

Observation does not necessarily establish broader semantic truth such as:

```text
"Requirement 42 is satisfied."
```

The distinction MUST be preserved.

---

# 21. Observation Source

Observation Claims SHOULD identify:

* observed system,
* observed object,
* observed revision or state where available,
* observation time,
* observing actor or process,
* relevant provenance.

---

# 22. Observation Time

The time at which a state was observed MUST remain distinguishable from the time during which the asserted state was valid.

Example:

```text
observedAt: 2026-08-30T14:00:00Z
```

does not necessarily mean:

```text
validFrom: 2026-08-30T14:00:00Z
```

---

# 23. Inference

An inference Claim is derived from other information rather than directly observed.

Examples:

```text
"These two files likely represent the same Subject."
```

```text
"This requirement is probably implemented by module X."
```

```text
"This failure is likely caused by configuration drift."
```

Inference MUST remain distinguishable from observation and confirmation.

---

# 24. Inference Inputs

An inferred Claim SHOULD retain provenance sufficient to identify the Claims, Evidence, observations, or computations from which it was derived.

Conceptually:

```text
C1 ──┐
C2 ──┼──► inference process ──► C3
E1 ──┘
```

---

# 25. Inference Confidence

An inference MAY carry a confidence measure.

Confidence SHOULD indicate the system's uncertainty about the proposition.

Confidence MUST NOT encode:

* authority,
* permission,
* approval,
* Evidence validity,
* governance status.

---

# 26. Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a Claim intentionally maintained as a proposition requiring investigation.

Example:

```text
"Graph traversal will provide materially better impact analysis than indexed search."
```

Monad SHOULD allow hypotheses to remain first-class so that:

* Evidence may accumulate,
* experiments may test them,
* competing hypotheses may coexist,
* conclusions may later strengthen, weaken, reject, or supersede them.

---

# 27. Hypothesis Lifecycle

A hypothesis MAY transition through states such as:

```text
proposed
under investigation
supported
weakened
rejected
superseded
unresolved
```

Lifecycle state MUST remain distinct from truth value.

---

# 28. Normative Claim

A normative Claim expresses what ought to be true rather than what currently is true.

Requirements are examples of normative engineering Claims.

For example:

```text
"The system MUST preserve stable Subject identity across file moves."
```

is not equivalent to the observational Claim:

```text
"The current implementation preserves Subject identity across file moves."
```

Monad MUST preserve this distinction.

---

# 29. Descriptive Claim

A descriptive Claim asserts something about actual or believed state.

Example:

```text
"The current implementation stores the graph in SQLite."
```

A descriptive Claim MAY be current, historical, inferred, or observational.

---

# 30. Claim Scope

A Claim MAY apply only within a defined scope.

Examples:

```text
repository
workspace
branch
environment
deployment
release
organization
component
revision
time period
```

Scope MUST be explicit where omission could materially change meaning.

---

# 31. Environment Scope

The following Claims are not necessarily equivalent:

```text
Service A uses PostgreSQL in development.
```

```text
Service A uses PostgreSQL in production.
```

Environment-scoped facts MUST NOT be collapsed.

---

# 32. Revision Scope

Claims MAY be revision-specific.

Example:

```text
"Specification S is approved."
```

may apply only to revision `R3`.

When revision matters, the Claim MUST identify or otherwise bind to the relevant revision.

---

# 33. Claim Valid Time

Where temporal semantics matter, a Claim SHOULD support the interval during which the asserted proposition is considered valid.

Conceptually:

```text
validFrom
validUntil
```

This specification does not require every Claim to carry explicit time intervals.

---

# 34. Claim Recorded Time

Monad SHOULD preserve when a Claim became known or was recorded separately from when the proposition was valid.

This distinction enables retroactive knowledge.

Example:

```text
Outage began: 10:00
Incident Claim recorded: 10:07
```

---

# 35. Historical Claims

Claims that are no longer currently valid MAY remain historically true.

Example:

```text
"Release 1.4 was deployed to production."
```

may have been true during one interval even though Release 1.5 is now deployed.

Historical Claims SHOULD remain queryable.

---

# 36. Claim Lifecycle

Claims MAY possess lifecycle state.

Possible lifecycle concepts include:

```text
candidate
active
accepted
contested
superseded
invalidated
withdrawn
historical
archived
```

The final vocabulary is a schema concern.

Lifecycle state MUST NOT be used as a hidden substitute for Evidence or authority.

---

# 37. Claim Provenance

Every materially consequential Claim SHOULD have provenance sufficient to answer:

> Where did this assertion come from?

Provenance MAY identify:

* producer,
* source artifact,
* source native system,
* transformation,
* derivation inputs,
* observation operation,
* AI model invocation,
* human action,
* import,
* reconciliation run.

---

# 38. Claim Producer

The producer is the actor or process that produced or recorded the Claim.

Possible producers include:

```text
human
AI model
compiler
scanner
integration
test runner
build system
deployment system
reconciliation engine
importer
policy engine
verification engine
```

Producer identity MUST remain distinct from Claim authority.

---

# 39. Source and Producer

The producer of a Claim MAY differ from its source.

Example:

```text
Source:
    GitHub API

Producer:
    Monad GitHub adapter
```

Likewise:

```text
Source:
    requirements.mdx

Producer:
    claim extraction engine
```

This distinction SHOULD be retained where materially useful.

---

# 40. Requested and Resolved Origin

Where a Claim is produced through resolution or indirection, provenance SHOULD distinguish:

```text
requested origin
```

from:

```text
resolved origin
```

For example:

```text
requested:
    main branch

resolved:
    commit abc123
```

This supports reproducibility and historical explanation.

---

# 41. Claim Authority

A Claim MAY possess or derive authority within a defined scope.

Authority answers:

> Is this source legitimately entitled to establish this state?

Authority does not answer:

> Is the proposition factually correct?

Both dimensions MAY matter independently.

---

# 42. Authoritative but False Claims

Monad MUST permit representation of a Claim that was authoritative but later found incorrect.

For example:

```text
authoritative deployment record:
    deployment successful

later Evidence:
    service never became healthy
```

The original authoritative Claim remains part of the historical engineering record.

---

# 43. Correct but Unauthorized Claims

Monad MUST also permit representation of a correct Claim produced without authority.

For example:

```text
Engineer:
    "Production is running commit X."
```

The Claim may be factually correct even if the engineer lacks authority to establish canonical deployment state.

---

# 44. Claim Acceptance

Monad MAY maintain a distinction between:

```text
Claim exists
```

and:

```text
Claim accepted as current canonical state
```

Acceptance MUST be determined through appropriate authority, policy, Evidence, reconciliation, or domain rules.

It MUST NOT arise merely from insertion order.

---

# 45. Claim Priority

Monad MUST NOT resolve competing Claims using simplistic precedence such as:

```text
latest timestamp wins
```

unless a domain specification explicitly establishes that rule.

Priority may depend on:

* authority,
* source,
* scope,
* Evidence,
* validity period,
* revision,
* freshness,
* policy.

---

# 46. Claim Freshness

A Claim MAY have freshness requirements.

For example:

```text
"Deployment X is healthy."
```

may become stale quickly.

By contrast:

```text
"ADR-0042 was accepted on March 1."
```

may remain historically stable.

Freshness MUST be interpreted according to Claim semantics.

---

# 47. Stale Claims

A Claim that is stale MUST NOT automatically be treated as false.

Staleness means the available assertion is no longer sufficiently current for a particular use.

---

# 48. Evidence Relationship

Claims MAY be related to Evidence.

Evidence relationships SHOULD indicate how Evidence bears on a Claim.

Possible semantics include:

```text
supports
contradicts
qualifies
weakens
corroborates
fails-to-support
```

The exact vocabulary belongs to the Evidence or relationship specification.

---

# 49. Evidence Does Not Become Claim State Automatically

Adding supporting Evidence MUST NOT automatically transform a Claim into truth.

Likewise, contradictory Evidence MUST NOT automatically delete the Claim.

Evidence contributes to assessment.

It does not erase the epistemic record.

---

# 50. Multiple Evidence Items

A Claim MAY have many supporting or contradicting Evidence items.

Monad SHOULD preserve individual Evidence identity and provenance rather than reducing all Evidence to a single opaque confidence number.

---

# 51. Evidence Independence

Multiple Evidence items originating from one underlying source MUST NOT automatically be treated as independent corroboration.

For example:

```text
dashboard
API result
generated report
```

may all derive from the same database row.

Provenance SHOULD allow that dependence to be detected.

---

# 52. Claim Support State

Monad MAY derive an assessment of Claim support.

Possible conceptual states include:

```text
unsupported
weakly supported
supported
strongly supported
contradicted
mixed
unknown
```

Such a state MUST be understood as a derived assessment, not the Claim itself.

---

# 53. Conflicting Claims

Monad MUST support multiple Claims whose propositions cannot all be simultaneously true within the same relevant scope and time.

For example:

```text
C1:
    production release = 1.4

C2:
    production release = 1.5
```

If both concern the same environment and valid time, they may conflict.

Both Claims MUST remain inspectable until reconciliation resolves the state.

---

# 54. Claim Conflict ≠ Data Corruption

Conflicting Claims are not necessarily a storage error.

They may represent real disagreement among:

* native systems,
* actors,
* observations,
* historical records,
* inferred state,
* authoritative sources.

Monad's semantic model MUST be capable of representing such disagreement intentionally.

---

# 55. Conflict Detection

Implementations SHOULD detect materially incompatible Claims when enough structured semantics exist.

Conflict detection MAY consider:

* same Subject,
* incompatible predicate values,
* overlapping temporal scope,
* overlapping environment scope,
* cardinality constraints,
* domain invariants.

---

# 56. Conflict Resolution

Resolving a Claim conflict MAY result in:

* one Claim accepted,
* another rejected,
* temporal separation,
* scope separation,
* both Claims retained as unresolved,
* corrected Claim,
* new higher-authority Claim,
* discovery that the Claims concerned different Subjects.

Resolution MUST preserve provenance.

---

# 57. Competing Hypotheses

Multiple hypotheses SHOULD be allowed to coexist without premature resolution.

For example:

```text
H1:
    failures caused by network saturation

H2:
    failures caused by connection pool exhaustion

H3:
    failures caused by provider throttling
```

Investigation may gather Evidence bearing differently on each.

---

# 58. Claim Equivalence

Two Claims MAY express semantically equivalent propositions.

Equivalent propositions do not necessarily imply identical Claim identity.

Monad MAY represent:

```text
same proposition
different Claim
```

where provenance differs.

---

# 59. Claim Deduplication

Implementations MAY identify duplicate Claims for usability or storage efficiency.

Deduplication MUST NOT destroy materially distinct:

* provenance,
* authority,
* source,
* temporal scope,
* revision scope,
* epistemic status.

---

# 60. Claim Aggregation

Monad MAY derive aggregate Claims from multiple lower-level Claims.

Example:

```text
test A passed
test B passed
test C passed
        ↓
"verification suite passed"
```

The aggregate Claim MUST preserve derivation provenance.

---

# 61. Derived Claims

A derived Claim is generated through a computation, rule, transformation, or inference over other semantic state.

Derived Claims MUST remain distinguishable from source Claims.

The system SHOULD be able to answer:

> From what was this Claim derived?

---

# 62. Materialized Claims

Derived Claims MAY be materialized for:

* performance,
* querying,
* reporting,
* projections.

Materialization MUST NOT silently elevate derived Claims into independently authoritative facts.

---

# 63. Reconstructable Claims

Where practical, derived Claims SHOULD be reconstructable from authoritative inputs and derivation rules.

If reconstruction is impossible, the Claim SHOULD retain enough provenance to explain why.

---

# 64. Claim Correction

When a Claim is found incorrect, Monad SHOULD preserve the original Claim and represent the correction explicitly.

Conceptually:

```text
C1:
    Package version = 2.4

C2:
    Package version = 2.5

C2 corrects C1
```

The system MUST NOT silently rewrite historical assertions when provenance matters.

---

# 65. Claim Retraction

An actor MAY retract a previously made Claim.

Retraction means:

> The producer no longer stands behind this Claim.

Retraction does not mean:

> The Claim never existed.

---

# 66. Claim Invalidation

A Claim MAY become invalid because:

* its source was corrupted,
* its derivation was flawed,
* its scope was incorrect,
* its Evidence was invalidated,
* its identity binding was wrong,
* its predicate interpretation changed.

Invalidation MUST preserve historical traceability.

---

# 67. Claim Supersession

A Claim MAY supersede another Claim.

Supersession SHOULD preserve:

* predecessor identity,
* successor identity,
* reason,
* provenance,
* applicable temporal boundary.

Supersession MUST NOT imply that the earlier Claim was necessarily false.

---

# 68. Revision of a Claim

A Claim MAY evolve through revisions while retaining Claim identity when the underlying assertion remains materially continuous.

A material change in proposition MAY require a new Claim identity.

Implementations MUST distinguish:

```text
revision of same Claim
```

from:

```text
new Claim
```

---

# 69. Claim Revision Binding

Where governance or verification applies to a specific Claim revision, the relevant operation MUST bind to that revision rather than an unqualified mutable Claim.

This prevents approval of one proposition revision from being silently applied to another.

---

# 70. Negative Claims

Monad MUST support explicit negative propositions.

Example:

```text
"Service A does not depend on Service B."
```

A negative Claim is distinct from absence of a positive Claim.

---

# 71. Absence of Claim

The absence of:

```text
A depends on B
```

MUST NOT automatically imply:

```text
A does not depend on B
```

unless the relevant domain uses a closed-world assumption.

Closed-world assumptions MUST be explicit.

---

# 72. Unknown Values

Claims MAY explicitly state unknown or unresolved values.

Unknown MUST remain distinguishable from:

* null,
* false,
* empty,
* not applicable,
* inaccessible,
* not observed.

---

# 73. Claim Completeness

Monad MUST NOT imply that its Claim set is a complete description of reality unless a domain contract establishes completeness.

The model generally represents what Monad currently knows or records.

---

# 74. Claim Canonicality

A Claim MAY be designated canonical for a particular semantic scope.

Canonicality means:

> This is the Claim Monad currently uses as the governing assertion for this scope.

Canonicality does not mean:

* universal truth,
* immutable truth,
* historically exclusive truth.

---

# 75. Canonical Claim Selection

Canonical Claim selection MUST be governed by explicit semantics.

Selection MAY depend on:

* authority,
* policy,
* Evidence,
* temporal validity,
* scope,
* source ownership,
* reconciliation.

It MUST NOT be determined solely by storage order.

---

# 76. Canonical and Historical Claims

Canonical current state MUST NOT erase noncanonical historical Claims.

Monad SHOULD support queries over both:

```text
current accepted Claims
```

and:

```text
all recorded Claims
```

---

# 77. Native-System Claims

Native systems MAY produce Claims about native state.

Examples:

```text
GitHub:
    issue state = closed
```

```text
CI:
    build = passed
```

```text
Kubernetes:
    deployment replicas available = 3
```

Such Claims SHOULD retain native semantics.

---

# 78. Native Claim ≠ Semantic Conclusion

Monad MUST NOT automatically infer:

```text
GitHub issue closed
```

means:

```text
requirement satisfied
```

or:

```text
CI passed
```

means:

```text
release verified
```

Higher-level semantic conclusions require explicit derivation.

---

# 79. Native Authority

A native system may be authoritative for particular native Claims.

For example:

```text
Git is authoritative for commit content.
```

That authority does not automatically extend to unrelated semantic Claims.

Authority remains scoped.

---

# 80. Imported Claims

Imported Claims MUST retain source provenance where available.

Import MUST NOT silently convert:

```text
external assertion
```

into:

```text
Monad-confirmed canonical fact
```

unless applicable governance explicitly permits that promotion.

---

# 81. Filesystem-Derived Claims

The filesystem MAY produce Claims such as:

```text
file exists
file path = X
file content hash = Y
```

It does not automatically establish:

```text
document is authoritative
requirement is approved
specification is current
```

Those are separate Claims.

---

# 82. Git-Derived Claims

Git MAY support Claims concerning:

* revisions,
* commits,
* authorship metadata,
* repository state,
* history.

Git history MUST NOT be treated as complete semantic history.

---

# 83. AI-Generated Claims

AI systems MAY generate Claims.

Every consequential AI-generated Claim SHOULD preserve:

* AI producer identity,
* model/provider where materially relevant,
* invocation provenance,
* source context,
* epistemic status,
* confidence if available,
* applicable authority.

---

# 84. AI Claim Authority

AI generation MUST NOT automatically confer authority.

A model's technical capability to produce a Claim does not authorize the model to establish that Claim as canonical state.

---

# 85. AI Inference

AI-generated semantic interpretation SHOULD default to an epistemic state appropriate to inference unless directly reporting an observation supplied by a trustworthy source.

Examples include:

```text
"These requirements appear contradictory."
```

```text
"This code likely implements REQ-123."
```

```text
"This ADR appears superseded."
```

Such assertions require confirmation or further Evidence according to policy.

---

# 86. AI Hallucination Containment

The Claim model SHOULD allow unsupported AI assertions to remain identifiable as AI-generated Claims rather than contaminating canonical semantic state.

This is a foundational safety property of the semantic system.

---

# 87. Human-Generated Claims

Human Claims SHOULD retain provenance where materially consequential.

Human authorship does not make a Claim automatically:

* correct,
* authoritative,
* verified,
* current.

Human and AI Claims SHOULD be governed through the same semantic distinctions.

---

# 88. Requirement Claims

Requirements express normative Claims.

Monad SHOULD preserve the distinction between:

```text
Requirement exists
Requirement accepted
Requirement currently applicable
Requirement satisfied
Requirement verified
```

These are separate propositions.

---

# 89. Decision Claims

A decision artifact may produce Claims such as:

```text
Decision D selected PostgreSQL.
Decision D rejected SQLite.
Decision D supersedes Decision C.
```

The existence of the document alone does not necessarily establish all such Claims unless they are semantically extracted or otherwise represented.

---

# 90. Specification Claims

Specifications contain normative and descriptive Claims.

Monad MAY formalize selected specification clauses as Claims where doing so improves:

* traceability,
* verification,
* impact analysis,
* policy enforcement.

It SHOULD NOT require every explanatory sentence to become a structured Claim.

---

# 91. Verification Claims

Verification may produce Claims such as:

```text
REQ-123 verified against revision abc123
```

or:

```text
Acceptance criterion AC-7 failed
```

Such Claims MUST preserve the verification scope and Evidence on which they depend.

---

# 92. Release Claims

Release records MAY assert:

```text
Release R contains revision X.
Release R passed verification V.
Release R is approved for production.
```

These propositions MUST remain distinguishable.

---

# 93. Execution Claims

Execution may produce observational Claims such as:

```text
operation started
operation completed
command exited 0
artifact published
deployment requested
```

Execution success MUST NOT automatically produce a Claim that intended system state was verified.

---

# 94. Reconciliation Claims

Reconciliation MAY produce Claims such as:

```text
native state agrees with canonical state
native state diverges
binding is stale
observed state is unauthorized
identity is ambiguous
```

These Claims SHOULD preserve the observations and rules from which they were derived.

---

# 95. Policy Claims

Policy evaluation MAY produce Claims such as:

```text
operation permitted
operation denied
approval required
Evidence insufficient
policy not applicable
```

These are outputs of policy evaluation.

They MUST NOT be confused with the policy definitions themselves.

---

# 96. Claim Relationships

Claims MAY participate in typed relationships such as:

```text
supports
contradicts
corrects
supersedes
derived-from
refines
weakens
qualifies
explains
caused-by
corroborates
```

The Relationship Specification defines the normative relationship model.

---

# 97. Claim-to-Claim Support

A Claim MAY support another Claim without being Evidence itself.

Example:

```text
C1:
    database connection count exceeded 500

C2:
    connection pool exhaustion contributed to outage
```

C1 may support C2.

Whether C1 also constitutes Evidence depends on the Evidence model and provenance.

---

# 98. Claim Causality

Causal Claims MUST remain distinguishable from:

* correlation,
* chronology,
* dependency,
* rationale,
* association.

Monad MUST NOT infer causality solely from temporal ordering.

---

# 99. Claim Strengthening

New Evidence or analysis MAY strengthen a Claim without changing its proposition.

Strengthening SHOULD be modeled as changed assessment, Evidence relationships, or epistemic state rather than silently rewriting the Claim proposition.

---

# 100. Claim Weakening

A Claim MAY become less supported without being fully rejected.

Monad SHOULD preserve intermediate epistemic states where useful.

---

# 101. Claim Refinement

A Claim MAY be refined into a more precise Claim.

Example:

```text
C1:
    "Performance is slow."
```

refined by:

```text
C2:
    "p95 API latency exceeds 800 ms under workload W."
```

Refinement MUST NOT imply identity equivalence between C1 and C2.

---

# 102. Claim Generalization

A more general Claim MAY be derived from multiple specific Claims.

Example:

```text
three provider-specific failures
        ↓
"current retry strategy is insufficient"
```

Derivation MUST remain inspectable.

---

# 103. Claim Partitioning

A Claim discovered to be overly broad MAY be replaced by multiple narrower Claims.

Example:

```text
"Component A is secure."
```

may be decomposed into Claims concerning:

* authentication,
* authorization,
* encryption,
* dependency vulnerabilities,
* secrets handling.

The original broad Claim SHOULD remain historically traceable.

---

# 104. Claim Granularity

Monad SHOULD encourage Claim granularity sufficient to enable useful:

* Evidence relationships,
* conflict detection,
* verification,
* traceability.

It SHOULD NOT force fragmentation so fine that the knowledge system becomes impractical.

---

# 105. Claim Economy

A Claim should earn first-class representation when explicit semantic treatment materially improves:

* reasoning,
* traceability,
* governance,
* verification,
* reconciliation,
* AI context,
* historical understanding.

Not every sentence deserves a Claim node.

---

# 106. Claim Extraction

Monad MAY extract candidate Claims from unstructured artifacts.

Extraction MUST preserve:

* source location,
* source artifact,
* extraction process,
* inferred status.

Automatically extracted candidate Claims MUST NOT silently become canonical.

---

# 107. Claim Parsing Failure

If Monad cannot reliably interpret a proposition, it SHOULD preserve the original assertion rather than invent a structured meaning.

Lossless ambiguity is preferable to false precision.

---

# 108. Semantic Normalization

Monad MAY normalize structured Claims into canonical predicate/value representations.

Normalization MUST NOT discard materially significant source semantics.

Original representation SHOULD remain traceable where normalization may be lossy.

---

# 109. Lossy Transformation

Any transformation that changes or omits material Claim meaning MUST be identifiable as lossy.

Lossy normalized Claims MUST preserve provenance to their source assertion.

---

# 110. Claim Portability

Canonical Claim semantics SHOULD remain portable across conforming Monad implementations.

Portability SHOULD preserve:

* Claim identity,
* proposition,
* Subject references,
* provenance,
* temporal scope,
* authority references,
* Evidence relationships,
* lifecycle.

---

# 111. Provider-Specific Claims

Provider-specific Claims MAY preserve native extension data.

Portable representations MUST NOT pretend provider-specific semantics have universal equivalents when they do not.

---

# 112. Claim Serialization

The serialized representation of a Claim MUST identify the schema or version necessary for correct interpretation.

Importers MUST NOT silently reinterpret incompatible Claim forms.

---

# 113. Claim Import Conflicts

Importing Claims into an existing system MAY produce:

* duplicate Claims,
* equivalent Claims,
* conflicting Claims,
* identity collisions,
* unresolved references.

These conditions MUST be detected or explicitly preserved.

---

# 114. Claim Export

Scoped Claim exports SHOULD define whether they include:

* Claims,
* Subjects,
* referenced predicates,
* provenance,
* Evidence,
* related Claims,
* authority data,
* lifecycle history.

Incomplete dependency closure SHOULD be explicit.

---

# 115. Local-First Claims

Core Claim state MUST remain inspectable and queryable locally where the applicable Monad deployment operates local-first.

Remote service availability MUST NOT be required merely to interpret canonical Claim semantics.

---

# 116. Offline Claims

Actors MAY produce Claims while offline.

When synchronized later, Monad MUST support reconciliation of:

* duplicates,
* conflicts,
* stale Claims,
* divergent authority,
* identity changes.

---

# 117. Claim Integrity

Claim storage SHOULD provide mechanisms sufficient to detect unintended corruption.

Integrity controls MAY include:

* hashes,
* transactional persistence,
* append-only history,
* checksums,
* signatures.

Integrity does not prove proposition truth.

---

# 118. Signed Claims

A cryptographic signature MAY establish that a particular key signed particular Claim content.

It does not by itself establish:

* semantic truth,
* authority,
* Evidence sufficiency,
* current validity.

---

# 119. Claim Security

Access to Claims MAY be governed independently for:

* reading,
* creating,
* modifying,
* accepting,
* retracting,
* deleting,
* exporting.

Technical access MUST remain distinct from semantic authority.

---

# 120. Sensitive Claims

Claims MAY contain sensitive information.

Implementations SHOULD support appropriate:

* classification,
* access control,
* redaction,
* retention,
* export handling.

Redaction SHOULD preserve enough structure to indicate that information was intentionally withheld where appropriate.

---

# 121. Claim Retention

Retention policy SHOULD consider whether Claims participate in:

* decisions,
* approvals,
* releases,
* incidents,
* verification,
* governance,
* identity transitions,
* historical explanation.

Consequential historical Claims SHOULD generally not be destructively discarded without policy justification.

---

# 122. Claim Deletion

Deleting a Claim MUST NOT silently delete:

* Subjects,
* Evidence,
* provenance records,
* other Claims.

Relationship consequences MUST be explicit.

---

# 123. Claim Querying

The system SHOULD support queries such as:

* What Claims exist about Subject S?
* Which Claims are currently canonical?
* Which Claims conflict?
* Which Claims lack Evidence?
* Which Claims are stale?
* Which Claims are AI-generated?
* Which Claims are inferred?
* Which Claims are authoritative?
* Which Claims changed since revision R?
* Which Claims support this decision?
* Which Claims were invalidated?
* What did Monad believe at time T?

---

# 124. Epistemic Querying

Monad SHOULD support queries that distinguish:

```text
observed
inferred
asserted
confirmed
authoritative
supported
contradicted
unresolved
historical
```

This distinction is necessary for trustworthy human and AI reasoning over the system model.

---

# 125. Current-State Queries

A query for current state SHOULD return Claims selected under the applicable canonicality, authority, scope, freshness, and temporal rules.

It MUST NOT simply return the most recently inserted Claim.

---

# 126. Historical Queries

Monad SHOULD support queries equivalent to:

> What Claims were considered valid or canonical at time T?

where retained history permits.

Historical reconstruction MUST NOT use only present-day state if doing so would misrepresent past truth.

---

# 127. Provenance Queries

The system SHOULD support answering:

> Why does Monad contain this Claim?

and:

> From what sources was this Claim derived?

---

# 128. Impact Analysis

Claims MAY participate in impact analysis.

For example, changing:

```text
Claim:
    Component A depends on B
```

may affect:

* architecture,
* build ordering,
* release planning,
* verification,
* ownership,
* security analysis.

Impact analysis MUST distinguish explicit relationships from speculative inference.

---

# 129. Claim Reconciliation

Reconciliation SHOULD compare Claims against newly observed native state where those Claims concern externally observable facts.

Potential outcomes include:

```text
confirmed
divergent
stale
contradicted
unobservable
unknown
```

Reconciliation MUST NOT treat agreement as automatic correctness.

---

# 130. Claim Revalidation

Claims whose correctness depends on mutable external state MAY require periodic revalidation.

Revalidation frequency SHOULD be proportional to:

* volatility,
* consequence,
* freshness requirements,
* cost.

---

# 131. Claim Obsolescence

A Claim MAY become obsolete because the question it answers is no longer relevant.

Obsolescence MUST remain distinguishable from falsehood.

---

# 132. Claim Applicability

A true Claim may still be inapplicable to a particular operation.

Example:

```text
"Component X supports Windows."
```

may be true but irrelevant to a Linux-only release.

Applicability SHOULD remain contextual.

---

# 133. Context

Claims MAY require Context for correct interpretation.

Context MUST NOT silently become part of Subject identity.

Examples include:

```text
environment
workspace
tenant
branch
release
execution
actor
```

---

# 134. Context Loss

A Claim whose meaning depends on context MUST NOT be detached from that context during normalization, export, or projection.

For example:

```text
"status = ready"
```

is meaningless if the relevant lifecycle or environment scope is lost.

---

# 135. Claim Projection

Read models MAY project Claims into simpler forms.

Example:

```text
currentProductionRelease = "1.5"
```

Such projections MUST remain derivable or traceable to the Claims that produced them.

The projection is not a substitute for the underlying epistemic model.

---

# 136. UI Presentation

User interfaces SHOULD communicate material epistemic distinctions where omission could mislead users.

For example, UI SHOULD avoid presenting:

```text
AI-inferred dependency
```

as visually indistinguishable from:

```text
authoritatively declared dependency
```

when the difference affects engineering action.

---

# 137. AI Context Construction

When Claims are provided to AI systems, Monad SHOULD preserve enough metadata for the model or orchestration layer to distinguish:

* canonical Claims,
* historical Claims,
* inference,
* uncertainty,
* conflict,
* provenance,
* authority,
* Evidence status.

Flattening all Claims into undifferentiated prose risks epistemic corruption.

---

# 138. AI Output Ingestion

When AI output is ingested into Monad, candidate Claims SHOULD retain:

```text
producer = AI
status = inferred or proposed
```

unless a stronger epistemic classification is justified.

---

# 139. Claim Explanation

For consequential Claims, the system SHOULD support a human-understandable explanation including:

* proposition,
* source,
* producer,
* relevant Evidence,
* authority,
* status,
* temporal scope,
* conflicting Claims.

---

# 140. Claim Falsifiability

Where a Claim is intended to describe or predict observable engineering reality, it SHOULD be formulated precisely enough to identify what Evidence could challenge it.

Claims that cannot in principle be evaluated MAY still be useful, but their limitations SHOULD be explicit.

---

# 141. Semantic Truth Is Not Mandatory

Monad does not require an omniscient global truth resolver.

The system MUST remain useful when it possesses:

* incomplete knowledge,
* conflicting Claims,
* uncertain Evidence,
* external authority,
* stale observations.

The model's job is to represent epistemic reality faithfully enough to support engineering action.

---

# 142. Closed-World Domains

A domain MAY define a closed-world model in which absence of a positive Claim permits a negative conclusion.

Such behavior MUST be explicit in that domain's specification.

Monad MUST NOT assume closed-world reasoning globally.

---

# 143. Open-World Default

Unless otherwise specified, Monad SHOULD operate under an open-world assumption:

> Lack of knowledge is not proof of negation.

This is particularly important for:

* incomplete integrations,
* offline operation,
* partial imports,
* AI reasoning,
* external systems.

---

# 144. Claim Validation

Claim validation SHOULD occur in layers.

## Level 1 — Structural validity

Examples:

* required identity exists,
* proposition representation is valid,
* declared types are syntactically valid.

## Level 2 — Referential validity

Examples:

* Subject references resolve,
* predicate references resolve,
* referenced Claims exist.

## Level 3 — Type validity

Examples:

* predicate accepts this Subject type,
* value has expected type.

## Level 4 — Semantic validity

Examples:

* temporal interval is coherent,
* required scope is present,
* Claim does not violate domain invariants.

## Level 5 — Provenance validity

Examples:

* required origin is available,
* derivation inputs exist.

## Level 6 — Authority validity

Examples:

* producer is authorized to establish canonical state.

## Level 7 — Governance validity

Examples:

* required approvals or Evidence exist.

These validation levels MUST NOT be collapsed merely because one schema validator can check only structural constraints.

---

# 145. Claim Validation Failure

A structurally valid Claim may still be:

* semantically invalid,
* unauthorized,
* unsupported,
* contradictory,
* stale.

Validation results SHOULD identify these distinctions where materially important.

---

# 146. Storage Independence

This specification does not require Claims to be stored in:

* RDF,
* property graphs,
* relational tables,
* document stores,
* event stores,
* vector databases.

The persistence architecture MUST preserve the semantics defined here.

---

# 147. Graph Semantics

Claims may naturally form graph-shaped structures through:

* Subjects,
* predicates,
* Evidence,
* provenance,
* derivation,
* conflicts,
* relationships.

Graph-shaped semantics MUST NOT be interpreted as a requirement for graph-database persistence.

---

# 148. Search

Full-text and semantic search MAY locate Claims.

Search relevance MUST NOT be treated as epistemic authority.

---

# 149. Embeddings

Embeddings MAY assist:

* retrieval,
* similarity,
* clustering,
* duplicate detection,
* candidate relationship discovery.

Embedding proximity MUST NOT establish proposition equivalence or truth.

---

# 150. Claim Complexity Budget

The Claim model MUST remain simpler than the problems it solves.

Additional Claim structure SHOULD be introduced only when it materially improves:

* reasoning,
* traceability,
* verification,
* governance,
* reconciliation,
* AI reliability,
* historical explanation.

If conventional text, metadata, or search solves a use case adequately, first-class Claim formalization MAY be unnecessary.

---

# 151. Minimal Useful Claim

A minimal first-class Claim requires conceptually:

```text
Claim identity
Proposition
Provenance
```

When relevant, it SHOULD additionally identify:

```text
Subject
Scope
Time
Epistemic status
```

Everything beyond this minimum must justify its cost.

---

Conceptually:

```yaml

  id: claim:01K...

    id: subject:01K...
```

# 153. Observation Example

```yaml
claim:
  proposition:
    subject: github-issue:42

    value: closed

  type: observation

  provenance:
    source: GitHub
    producer: github-adapter
    observed_at: 2026-08-30T12:00:00Z
```

This Claim does **not** itself establish:

```text
REQ-42 is satisfied.
```

# 154. Inferred Claim Example

```yaml
claim:
  proposition:
    subject: requirement:REQ-42
    predicate: implemented-by
    object: source-module:auth

  type: inference

  confidence: 0.82

  provenance:
    producer: ai-analysis
    derived_from:
      - source-code observation
      - requirement text
```

This must remain distinguishable from a confirmed traceability relationship.

# 155. Conflict Example

```text
C1
  subject: Deployment PROD
  release: 1.4
  source: deployment database

C2
  subject: Deployment PROD
  release: 1.5
  source: cluster observation
```

Monad should record both.

Reconciliation determines whether:

* one is stale,
* the deployment database is wrong,
* the cluster is mid-rollout,
* the Claims refer to different scopes,
* another explanation exists.

---

# 156. Historical Claim Example

```text
C1
  production release = 1.4
  valid: 10:00–13:00

C2
  production release = 1.5
  valid: from 13:00
```

Both Claims may be true.

The difference is temporal scope.

---

# 157. Correction Example

```text
C1
  "Incident started at 14:32."

C2
  "Incident started at 14:27."

C2 corrects C1.
```

C1 remains historically inspectable.

---

# 158. AI Disagreement Example

```text
AI Claim A:
  Architecture requires distributed graph persistence.
  confidence: .78

Human Claim B:
  Architecture explicitly permits relational persistence.

Specification Claim C:
  Persistence mechanism is not normative.
```

Monad should not resolve this by counting votes.

Authority, provenance, and source semantics determine the useful interpretation.

---

# 159. Anti-Patterns

## 159.1 Claim as boolean field

```text
verified = true
```

without:

* scope,
* verification identity,
* revision,
* Evidence,
* provenance.

This destroys useful epistemic context.

---

## 159.2 Latest value wins

```text
ORDER BY created_at DESC LIMIT 1
```

used as universal truth resolution.

This is non-conforming where authority, time, or scope matters.

---

## 159.3 AI output as fact

An AI-generated assertion is inserted directly into canonical state without preserving inference status.

This collapses capability into authority.

---

## 159.4 Source authority as universal truth

A native system is treated as authoritative for every semantic question merely because it owns one native capability.

Authority must remain scoped.

---

## 159.5 Evidence embedded as prose only

Evidence is copied into Claim text without preserving independent identity or provenance.

This makes reassessment and reuse difficult.

---

## 159.6 Conflict deletion

A contradictory Claim is removed merely to make the database internally consistent.

This models convenience rather than engineering reality.

---

## 159.7 Unknown as false

Missing Claim:

```text
A depends on B
```

is interpreted as:

```text
A does not depend on B.
```

without a closed-world contract.

---

## 159.8 Confidence as authority

```text
confidence > .95
```

automatically permits a consequential mutation.

Confidence and authority are different dimensions.

---

## 159.9 Every sentence becomes a Claim

The system converts all prose into graph entities regardless of engineering value.

This produces semantic noise and unsustainable maintenance cost.

---

## 159.10 One canonical truth destroys history

When canonical state changes, all previous Claims are deleted.

This destroys historical truth and auditability.

---

# 160. Non-Requirements

This specification does **not** require:

* RDF,
* OWL,
* SPARQL,
* graph databases,
* formal logic for every Claim,
* proposition deduplication,
* global predicate ontology,
* machine interpretation of every sentence,
* confidence scores for every Claim,
* automatic truth resolution,
* AI-based Claim extraction,
* universal closed-world reasoning,
* permanent retention of every incidental assertion,
* one authoritative Claim for every predicate,
* converting existing engineering systems into semantic databases.

# 161. Conformance Scenario — Conflicting Native State

Given:

```text
CI says build passed.
Artifact registry says expected artifact is missing.
```

Then Monad MUST be capable of retaining both observations.

It MUST NOT silently conclude either:

```text
release succeeded
```

or:

```text
release failed
```

without the applicable higher-level semantics.

---

# 162. Conformance Scenario — AI Inference

Given an AI model infers:

```text
Requirement R is implemented by module M.
```

Then:

* the Claim MUST remain identifiable as AI-produced,
* inference MUST remain distinguishable from confirmation,
* confidence MUST NOT confer authority,
* applicable policy MAY require review.

---

# 163. Conformance Scenario — Historical Truth

Given:

```text
Release 1.0 was production at time T1.
Release 1.1 was production at time T2.
```

Then:

* both Claims MAY remain true,
* current-state queries MAY return only 1.1,
* historical queries for T1 MUST be capable of returning 1.0 where history is retained.

---

# 164. Conformance Scenario — Unknown Dependency

Given no Claim concerning whether A depends on B,

Then the system MUST NOT conclude:

```text
A does not depend on B
```

unless the domain explicitly defines complete dependency knowledge.

---

# 165. Conformance Scenario — Conflicting Authority

Given two authoritative sources assert incompatible values within overlapping scope,

Then:

* both Claims MUST remain representable,
* the system MUST surface the authority conflict,
* it MUST NOT resolve the conflict solely by timestamp.

---

# 166. Conformance Scenario — Correction

Given a Claim was accepted and later found incorrect,

Then:

* the prior Claim remains historically inspectable,
* a correction can be represented,
* provenance explains the change,
* derived current state may change.

---

# 167. Conformance Scenario — Claim Extraction

Given a parser extracts a structured Claim from prose,

Then:

* the source prose remains traceable,
* the extraction process has provenance,
* uncertain extraction remains inferred,
* failure to parse MUST NOT modify source meaning.

---

# 168. Conformance Scenario — Scope Difference

Given:

```text
C1: Service S uses SQLite in local development.
C2: Service S uses PostgreSQL in production.
```

Then the system MUST NOT report these as contradictory merely because the predicate and Subject match.

Scope matters.

---

# 169. Conformance Scenario — Temporal Conflict

Given:

```text
C1: status = active from January through March
C2: status = retired from April onward
```

Then the Claims are temporally compatible.

Conflict analysis MUST account for valid time where available.

---

# 170. Conformance Scenario — Duplicate Proposition

Given two independent scanners assert:

```text
Package P contains vulnerability V.
```

Then Monad MAY preserve two separate Claims sharing one proposition because their provenance is independently valuable.

# 173. Definition of Ready

This specification is ready for baseline acceptance when:

* Claim is clearly distinguished from truth,
* Claim is distinguished from Evidence,
* Claim is distinguished from provenance,
* Claim is distinguished from authority,
* observation and inference are differentiated,
* conflict can be represented without destructive resolution,
* historical and current truth are differentiated,
* Claim identity semantics are sufficiently defined,
* structured and opaque Claims are both supported,
* AI-generated Claims retain epistemic status,
* open schema decisions remain explicitly unresolved.

---

# 174. Definition of Done

The Claims specification is complete for the foundational specification baseline when:

* every semantic-kernel Claim obligation maps to a normative clause,
* downstream Evidence semantics can reference Claims without ambiguity,
* Provenance can identify Claim origin and derivation,
* Authority can govern Claim acceptance without owning Claim representation,
* Reconciliation can compare competing Claims,
* Verification can produce and evaluate Claims,
* AI interaction can safely create candidate Claims,
* schema work can proceed without redefining Claim semantics,
* conformance scenarios exist for the primary epistemic failure modes.

It also operationalizes boundaries established by:

| Requirement family | Claim concern                                           |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| `REQ-SEM-*`        | Claim representation, conflicts, epistemic distinctions |
| `REQ-EVD-*`        | Evidence support and contradiction                      |
| `REQ-PROV-*`       | Claim origin and derivation                             |
| `REQ-AUTH-*`       | authority over accepted assertions                      |
| `REQ-GOV-*`        | governed Claim mutation and acceptance                  |
| `REQ-REC-*`        | competing observed and canonical Claims                 |
| `REQ-INT-*`        | native-system observations                              |
| `REQ-PORT-*`       | portable Claim semantics                                |

Exact clause-level requirement mappings SHOULD be produced during the specification-baseline review.

# 177. Governing Principle

Monad should not pretend to know more than it knows.

It should preserve:

* what was asserted,
* who asserted it,
* where it came from,
* what supports it,
* what contradicts it,
* how certain it is,
* whether it is authoritative,
* when it applied,
* whether it remains current.

The governing principle is:

> **Represent assertions explicitly enough that disagreement, uncertainty, correction, and learning remain possible.**

A Claim model that turns every assertion into truth is too weak.

A Claim model that requires every sentence to become formal logic is too expensive.

Monad must preserve the useful space between those extremes.
