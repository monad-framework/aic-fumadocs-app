## What this page defines

[Concepts & Principles](/docs/system/concepts) defines authority, policy, capability, Actor, and Constitution conceptually.

[Architecture](/docs/system/architecture) establishes governance as a major architectural responsibility.

[Components](/docs/system/components) assigns policy evaluation and lifecycle enforcement to explicit components.

This page defines how those ideas compose into the governance model.

It answers:

> **Who or what may perform an engineering action, why is the action legitimate, what conditions apply, what evidence or approval is required, and how does Monad prevent technical capability from becoming unrestricted authority?**

This is **system governance**.

It should not be confused with organizational governance of the Monad open-source project itself.

---

# Governance as engineering architecture

In many software systems, governance exists mostly outside the software.

It appears as:

```text
team conventions
code-review expectations
approval rules
release checklists
security policies
tribal knowledge
```

Humans interpret those rules manually.

That works only while:

* everyone knows the rules,
* everyone remembers them,
* every actor understands the context,
* and automation remains relatively weak.

As engineering agents become capable of:

* modifying source,
* changing configuration,
* updating infrastructure,
* publishing packages,
* deploying services,
* creating releases,
* and modifying other engineering artifacts,

implicit governance becomes increasingly dangerous.

Monad therefore asks:

> **Which parts of engineering governance should become explicit enough for the system itself to understand and enforce?**

---

# The governance stack

Monad governance can be understood as several related layers:

```text
Constitution
      │
      ▼
Authority
      │
      ▼
Capabilities
      │
      ▼
Policies
      │
      ▼
Obligations
      │
      ▼
Approvals / Evidence
      │
      ▼
Authorized Operation
      │
      ▼
Execution
```

Each layer answers a different question.

```text
Constitution
  What foundational rules constrain the system?

Authority
  Why may this Actor govern or act in this scope?

Capability
  What can this Actor technically do?

Policy
  Is this operation permitted under these conditions?

Obligation
  What must also happen if it is permitted?

Approval
  Whose explicit authorization is required?

Evidence
  What must support the action or resulting claim?

Execution
  What side effect actually occurs?
```

These distinctions should remain explicit.

---

# Governance is not permission alone

A simple authorization system often asks:

```text
Can user X perform action Y?
```

Monad needs a richer question:

```text
May Actor X
perform Operation Y
against Subject Z
within Scope S
at Lifecycle State L
under Policy P
given Evidence E
with Approval A
against System Revision R?
```

The answer may not be merely:

```text
yes
```

or:

```text
no
```

It may instead be:

```text
permit

permit with obligations

require approval

require additional evidence

require narrower scope

require updated plan

deny

escalate
```

Governance therefore produces structured decisions rather than only access-control bits.

---

# The Constitution

The **Constitution** is the highest-order governance layer.

It defines the foundational rules within which all ordinary governance operates.

Potential constitutional concerns include:

* core semantic invariants,
* permitted foundational entity types,
* authority hierarchy,
* protected concepts,
* extension constraints,
* governance boundaries,
* constitutional versioning,
* and amendment procedure.

Conceptually:

```text
Constitution
     │
     │ constrains
     ▼
ordinary governance
     │
     ▼
ordinary system state
```

The Constitution should remain small.

If every routine engineering policy becomes constitutional, the architecture has failed to distinguish foundational rules from operational governance.

---

# Constitutional authority

Ordinary policy should not be able to rewrite constitutional constraints casually.

For example:

```text
ordinary policy:
  implementation agents may modify application code
```

must not imply:

```text
therefore implementation agents may modify
the rules that define implementation-agent authority
```

The system must distinguish authority over:

```text
ordinary engineering state
```

from authority over:

```text
governance rules themselves
```

and:

```text
constitutional rules
```

These are different scopes of power.

---

# Constitutional amendment

The Constitution must be able to evolve.

But constitutional evolution should follow stronger governance than ordinary change.

A conceptual amendment flow is:

```text
Amendment Proposal
      │
      ▼
Constitutional Impact Analysis
      │
      ▼
Compatibility Analysis
      │
      ▼
Required Review
      │
      ▼
Explicit Constitutional Approval
      │
      ▼
Migration Plan
      │
      ▼
Amendment
      │
      ▼
Verification
      │
      ▼
New Constitutional Version
```

The precise approval mechanism belongs in formal specification.

The system-level rule is:

> **Changing the rules governing the system must be distinguishable from operating within those rules.**

---

# Actor

An **Actor** is an identifiable participant capable of performing or requesting engineering activity.

Actors may include:

```text
human engineer
maintainer
reviewer
AI agent
automation
CI system
release service
external system
```

Governance begins with knowing:

```text
who or what is acting?
```

Anonymous technical capability should not silently become privileged authority.

---

# Actor identity

Governed operations should resolve to a meaningful Actor identity.

For example:

```text
Actor:
  human:alice
```

or:

```text
Actor:
  agent:implementation-agent-17
```

or:

```text
Actor:
  service:release-automation
```

The exact identifier form is a specification concern.

The architectural principle is that significant actions can be attributed to identifiable Actors.

---

# Actor versus role

An Actor is not the same thing as a role.

For example:

```text
Actor:
  Alice

Role:
  Maintainer
```

A role may contribute to authority.

But the action should still remain attributable to Alice.

Likewise:

```text
Actor:
  Agent-17

Role:
  Implementation Agent
```

The role describes expected function.

The Actor identifies who actually participated.

---

# Capability

A **Capability** describes what an Actor or system is technically able to do.

Examples:

```text
read source
modify source
create artifact
run tests
query system graph
change specification
approve review
publish package
deploy service
modify policy
```

Capabilities are important because different Actors may have very different technical abilities.

But:

> **Capability is not authority.**

An agent may technically possess a tool capable of deleting a repository.

That does not establish permission to do so.

---

# Capability declaration

Actors, adapters, agents, and extensions should be able to declare or expose their capabilities.

For example:

```text
Actor:
  implementation-agent

Capabilities:
  read-source
  modify-source
  run-tests
  create-patch
```

while another Actor might expose:

```text
Actor:
  release-service

Capabilities:
  create-release
  publish-package
  publish-container
```

Governance can then reason explicitly about what operations are even technically available.

---

# Capability is not delegation

Giving a component access to:

```text
GitHub credential
```

may technically allow many GitHub operations.

Monad should not treat that credential as equivalent to authorization for every operation it enables.

A better model is:

```text
credential
   enables technical capability

policy + authority
   constrain permissible use
```

This is especially important for agents and plugins.

---

# Authority

**Authority** answers:

> **Why is this Actor entitled to govern or perform this operation within this scope?**

Authority may originate from:

* constitutional rules,
* explicit delegation,
* role assignment,
* ownership,
* policy,
* lifecycle state,
* approved work scope,
* human authorization,
* or another authoritative system.

Authority should always be understood relative to scope.

---

# Scoped authority

Authority is not necessarily global.

For example:

```text
Actor A

may:
  modify source under packages/auth/**

may not:
  modify public API specifications
  change release policy
  amend the Constitution
```

This is stronger than simply declaring:

```text
Actor A = developer
```

Scoped authority can express:

```text
resource scope
operation scope
time scope
project scope
lifecycle scope
capability scope
```

---

# Delegation

Authority may be delegated.

For example:

```text
Maintainer
   │
   │ delegates bounded work
   ▼
Implementation Agent
```

The delegation should ideally define:

```text
Actor
Scope
Capabilities
Permitted Operations
Expiration
Required Verification
Escalation Conditions
```

For example:

```text
Delegate:
  implementation-agent

Scope:
  packages/auth/**

Permitted:
  modify-source
  run-tests

Not permitted:
  change-public-interface
  modify-policy
  publish-release

Expires:
  end of Work Package
```

This enables bounded autonomy.

---

# Authority should be revocable

Delegated authority should be removable or expire.

Possible triggers include:

```text
task completion
time expiration
scope change
policy change
security concern
Actor compromise
manual revocation
```

The system should not assume that authority, once granted, remains permanent.

---

# Authority chains

In some cases, authority may be derived through several layers.

For example:

```text
Constitution
     │
     ▼
Project Policy
     │
     ▼
Maintainer Authority
     │
     ▼
Work Package Authorization
     │
     ▼
Implementation Agent
```

A governed operation may therefore need to answer:

> Where did this Actor's authority come from?

This is analogous to provenance for information.

Authority itself may need provenance.

---

# Policy

A **Policy** defines a rule constraining actions or transitions under particular conditions.

Policies may express rules such as:

```text
Implementation agents may modify implementation
inside their authorized Work Package scope.
```

```text
Changes to public interfaces require compatibility review.
```

```text
Release publication requires successful verification.
```

```text
Constitutional changes require constitutional amendment authority.
```

Policies turn governance expectations into explicit system rules.

---

# Policy inputs

A policy decision may consider:

```text
Actor
Role
Capability
Requested Operation
Target
Scope
Lifecycle State
Current Revision
Risk
Environment
Evidence
Existing Approval
```

Conceptually:

```text
Actor
   +
Operation
   +
Target
   +
State
   +
Authority
   +
Policy Context
       │
       ▼
Policy Evaluation
```

---

# Policy outputs

A policy evaluation should support rich outcomes.

For example:

```text
PERMIT
```

```text
DENY
```

```text
PERMIT WITH OBLIGATIONS
```

```text
REQUIRE APPROVAL
```

```text
REQUIRE EVIDENCE
```

```text
REQUIRE ESCALATION
```

```text
REQUIRE REPLANNING
```

The system should be able to explain why.

---

# Explainable policy decisions

A policy decision should ideally answer:

```text
What was requested?

Which policy applied?

What decision was reached?

Why?

What obligations remain?

What authority was used?

What evidence or approval is missing?
```

For example:

```text
Decision:
  require-approval

Operation:
  modify-public-interface

Target:
  auth-api

Policy:
  POL-API-004

Reason:
  public interface changes require architecture review

Required approval:
  architecture-review
```

Governance should not feel arbitrary.

---

# Policy precedence

Multiple policies may apply simultaneously.

For example:

```text
Policy A:
  implementation agents may modify source

Policy B:
  public interfaces require review

Policy C:
  security-sensitive files require security approval
```

A change may satisfy all three scopes.

The system therefore needs explicit policy-composition rules.

Potential concerns include:

```text
deny precedence
more-specific scope
constitutional priority
explicit overrides
obligation accumulation
```

The exact evaluation semantics belong in policy specification.

---

# Conflicting policies

Policies may conflict.

For example:

```text
Policy A:
  Agent may modify schema.

Policy B:
  Schema modifications require human approval.
```

Monad should not silently choose whichever policy loaded last.

Instead, the conflict should be:

```text
detected
reported
resolved through defined precedence
or escalated
```

A governance system must itself be governable.

---

# Obligations

A policy may permit an action while attaching **obligations**.

For example:

```text
Operation:
  modify public API

Decision:
  permit

Obligations:
  update specification
  run compatibility suite
  obtain architecture review
  record change rationale
```

This is more expressive than:

```text
allowed / denied
```

because many engineering operations are conditionally legitimate.

---

# Obligation lifecycle

An obligation may be:

```text
required
satisfied
waived
failed
expired
```

An authorized operation should not be treated as fully complete if required obligations remain unresolved.

For example:

```text
implementation complete
       │
       ▼
compatibility verification pending
       │
       ▼
operation not yet complete
```

This links governance with lifecycle and verification.

---

# Approval

An **Approval** represents explicit authorization by an Actor with sufficient authority.

Examples may include:

```text
architecture approval
security approval
release approval
constitutional approval
exception approval
```

An approval should identify:

```text
approver
scope
subject
decision
time
revision
conditions
```

Approval should not be an unstructured checkbox detached from what was actually reviewed.

---

# Approval is revision-sensitive

Suppose:

```text
Plan v1
```

is approved.

Then the plan changes materially into:

```text
Plan v2
```

The previous approval should not automatically apply.

Conceptually:

```text
Approval
  binds to
  specific state / revision / scope
```

This prevents approval from becoming indefinitely reusable authority.

---

# Self-approval

Some governance contexts may prohibit an Actor from approving its own high-risk work.

For example:

```text
Implementation Agent
  performs change

Independent Reviewer
  approves change
```

This creates separation of duties.

The exact rules should depend on risk.

Monad should support independence requirements where they matter without making them universal ceremony.

---

# Evidence as governance input

Evidence can be required before an operation becomes valid.

For example:

```text
Release requires:
  successful acceptance tests
  security verification
  reproducibility check
```

or:

```text
Migration completion requires:
  source and target consistency evidence
```

Evidence therefore participates not only in verification but governance.

---

# Evidence versus approval

These are different.

```text
Evidence
  shows something about reality

Approval
  expresses authorized judgment
```

For example:

```text
Test result:
  compatibility suite passed
```

does not automatically imply:

```text
release approved
```

Likewise:

```text
maintainer approved release
```

does not prove:

```text
compatibility suite passed
```

Both may be required.

---

# Lifecycle gates

Governance often applies at state transitions.

For example:

```text
Specification

draft
   ↓
proposed
   ↓
accepted
   ↓
active
```

The transition:

```text
proposed → accepted
```

may require:

```text
review
authority
evidence
```

while:

```text
accepted → active
```

may require something else.

Lifecycle therefore creates natural governance gates.

---

# State-dependent authority

An Actor may be allowed to perform an operation in one lifecycle state but not another.

For example:

```text
draft specification
  editable by author

active specification
  requires change proposal

superseded specification
  historical; immutable except correction metadata
```

The operation cannot be evaluated without knowing the state.

---

# Risk-proportional governance

Not every change deserves the same process.

Monad should avoid governance that treats:

```text
fix typo
```

and:

```text
change authentication architecture
```

as equivalent.

A useful conceptual risk model might consider:

```text
blast radius
reversibility
security impact
public compatibility
data loss potential
authority sensitivity
constitutional significance
```

Governance can then increase as consequences increase.

---

# Risk tiers

A conceptual risk scale might look like:

```text
LOW
  local, reversible, well-tested

MODERATE
  bounded functional change

HIGH
  public contract, security, data, release

CRITICAL
  governance, constitutional, irreversible
```

The exact categories are a policy decision.

The governing principle is:

> **Governance should be proportional to consequence.**

---

# Bounded autonomy

One of Monad's primary goals is not to eliminate automation.

It is to enable **bounded autonomy**.

An agent may be given substantial freedom inside a clearly defined boundary.

For example:

```text
Agent may:

  inspect repository
  modify implementation
  add tests
  run validation
  create patch

within:

  Work Package WP-042

but may not:

  change requirements
  modify architecture
  publish release
  amend policy
```

This gives the agent useful independence without unrestricted authority.

---

# Autonomy envelope

A useful autonomy definition may include:

```text
Actor
Scope
Capabilities
Policies
Maximum Risk
Allowed Side Effects
Required Evidence
Escalation Conditions
Expiration
```

Conceptually:

```text
Autonomy Envelope
      │
      ├── where may I act?
      ├── what may I do?
      ├── how risky may the work be?
      ├── what must I verify?
      └── when must I stop and ask?
```

This is a more useful model than simply describing an agent as:

```text
autonomous
```

---

# Escalation

An Actor should be able to determine when it lacks authority.

For example:

```text
Requested:
  modify auth API

Agent Authority:
  implementation changes only

Detected:
  specification change required

Result:
  escalation required
```

A well-governed Actor should not interpret missing authority as a challenge to work around.

It should request additional authority or human decision.

---

# Protected Subjects

Some Subjects may require stronger governance.

Examples might include:

```text
Constitution
security policy
public schema
public API
migration history
release signing configuration
credential policy
```

A protected Subject can require:

* stronger Actor authority,
* mandatory review,
* restricted capabilities,
* higher evidence requirements,
* or complete prohibition for certain Actor classes.

---

# Protected operations

Protection may apply not only to Subjects but operations.

For example:

```text
read
  widely allowed

modify
  restricted

delete
  strongly restricted

supersede
  requires review

publish
  release authority required
```

Governance therefore evaluates:

```text
Actor + Operation + Subject + Scope
```

rather than Subject alone.

---

# Invariants

An **Invariant** is a condition that must remain true.

Some invariants are constitutional.

Others belong to specific domains.

Examples might include:

```text
Every active Representation resolves to a known Subject.

An active Specification cannot be superseded by itself.

A released artifact must reference verification evidence.

An Actor cannot exercise a Capability outside its delegated scope.
```

Governance can prevent operations that would violate invariants.

---

# Precondition versus invariant

These concepts differ.

A **precondition** is required for a particular operation.

An **invariant** should remain true across relevant system states.

For example:

```text
Precondition:
  expected repository revision matches current revision
```

versus:

```text
Invariant:
  confirmed Subject identities remain unique within scope
```

Governance may enforce both.

---

# Exceptions

No policy system can predict every legitimate exceptional circumstance.

Monad therefore needs explicit exception handling.

An exception should not mean:

```text
ignore policy
```

It should mean:

```text
policy normally prohibits this

an authorized Actor explicitly approved
a bounded exception

the exception has scope
reason
duration
conditions
and provenance
```

---

# Exception record

A useful exception may identify:

```text
policy
Actor requesting exception
approver
scope
reason
risk
expiration
required mitigation
```

For example:

```text
Policy:
  POL-RELEASE-004

Exception:
  skip benchmark threshold

Reason:
  benchmark infrastructure unavailable

Scope:
  release candidate rc3 only

Approved by:
  release-authority

Mitigation:
  benchmark required before stable release
```

Exceptions should be inspectable rather than invisible workarounds.

---

# Emergency authority

Some systems may require emergency operations.

For example:

```text
revoke compromised credential
disable deployment
rollback release
```

Emergency paths may legitimately bypass normal timing or review requirements.

But they should still preserve:

```text
Actor
reason
scope
operation
evidence
time
post-action review
```

Emergency authority should be powerful and narrow.

---

# Break-glass operations

A break-glass mechanism may allow exceptional privileged action under tightly controlled conditions.

Conceptually:

```text
Normal policy denies operation
       │
       ▼
Emergency condition declared
       │
       ▼
Break-glass authority invoked
       │
       ▼
Operation performed
       │
       ▼
Mandatory evidence + audit
       │
       ▼
Post-action review
```

A break-glass path should not become a convenient bypass for ordinary work.

---

# Human authority

Humans retain important governance responsibilities.

Monad does not assume all human decisions should eventually be automated.

Examples that may remain human-centered include:

* strategic tradeoffs,
* acceptance of new risk,
* constitutional amendments,
* irreversible decisions,
* organizational responsibility,
* and decisions where values matter more than technical optimization.

The architecture should represent human authority explicitly enough for machines to know when they must defer.

---

# AI authority

AI agents may receive substantial operational authority.

But their authority should be:

* explicit,
* scoped,
* revocable,
* inspectable,
* policy-bound,
* and proportional to risk.

For example:

```text
AI agent
  can create implementation patch

AI agent
  cannot approve its own architecture change
```

or:

```text
AI agent
  can merge low-risk dependency update
  after required verification passes
```

The exact delegation policy can vary by organization or project.

The system needs the mechanisms to express it.

---

# Human and machine governance use the same foundation

Humans and agents need not have identical policies.

But governance should use the same underlying concepts:

```text
Actor
Authority
Capability
Scope
Policy
Approval
Evidence
Lifecycle
```

This avoids building:

```text
one governance system for humans
another unrelated system for AI
```

where the two drift apart.

---

# Review

A **Review** evaluates an artifact, plan, change, or result.

Review may serve several functions:

```text
technical scrutiny
independence
risk assessment
policy satisfaction
approval input
evidence generation
```

A review should remain distinct from approval.

A reviewer may conclude:

```text
technically sound
```

without possessing authority to approve release.

---

# Review types

Potential review categories include:

```text
domain review
architecture review
security review
specification review
implementation review
verification review
release review
constitutional review
```

Different policies may require different kinds of review depending on the change.

---

# Review independence

Some reviews gain value from independence.

For example:

```text
Actor A
  creates implementation

Actor B
  verifies critical security behavior
```

The policy system may require that:

```text
A != B
```

for specific high-risk operations.

This need not apply to routine low-risk work.

---

# Governance and planning

Planning and governance are tightly connected but distinct.

Planning answers:

```text
What should be done?
```

Governance answers:

```text
May this plan be executed?
Under what conditions?
```

Conceptually:

```text
Intent
  ↓
Plan
  ↓
Impact
  ↓
Policy Evaluation
  ↓
Authorization
  ↓
Execution
```

A plan should be inspectable before it becomes action.

---

# Governance and execution

Execution must not independently grant itself authority.

The Execution Component should receive a governance result that establishes:

```text
operation
scope
Actor
authorization
obligations
expected state
```

Conceptually:

```text
Policy Decision
      │
      ▼
Authorized Operation
      │
      ▼
Execution Boundary
```

Execution may still reject the operation if preconditions are no longer valid.

---

# Governance and verification

Governance may define **what must be verified**.

Verification determines **whether that condition was satisfied**.

For example:

```text
Policy:
  public API changes require compatibility verification
```

Verification:

```text
Compatibility Suite:
  passed
```

Governance can then recognize the obligation as satisfied.

---

# Governance and provenance

Governance decisions should be traceable.

For important operations, Monad should be able to answer:

```text
Who requested this?

Who authorized it?

Which policy applied?

Which evidence was considered?

What obligations existed?

What operation was actually performed?

What was the outcome?
```

This creates governance provenance.

---

# Governance and identity

Authority cannot be reliable if Actor or Subject identity is ambiguous.

For example:

```text
policy permits maintainer to modify Subject A
```

is meaningless if Monad cannot determine which thing is Subject A.

Governance therefore depends heavily on trustworthy identity resolution.

---

# Governance and authority of information

Governance is not only about actions.

It also concerns information.

For example:

```text
Which artifact may define required behavior?

Which source may declare a release complete?

Which system owns runtime truth?

Which claim may supersede another?
```

The authority model therefore governs both:

```text
action authority
```

and:

```text
information authority
```

These related concerns should remain distinguishable.

---

# Governance hierarchy

A simplified hierarchy might look like:

```text
CONSTITUTION
    │
    ▼
SYSTEM-WIDE POLICY
    │
    ▼
DOMAIN POLICY
    │
    ▼
PROJECT / WORKSPACE POLICY
    │
    ▼
WORK-SCOPE AUTHORITY
    │
    ▼
OPERATION-SPECIFIC AUTHORIZATION
```

Lower levels operate inside constraints imposed by higher levels.

A Work Package cannot legitimately authorize an operation prohibited by the Constitution.

---

# Policy scope

Policies may apply at many scopes.

Examples:

```text
global
workspace
project
repository
domain
Subject type
individual Subject
Actor type
individual Actor
operation
environment
release channel
```

Specificity matters.

The system needs enough scope semantics to avoid applying every rule globally.

---

# Policy inheritance

Some scopes may inherit policy from broader scopes.

For example:

```text
Organization Policy
        ↓
Project Policy
        ↓
Repository Policy
        ↓
Subject-specific Policy
```

A narrower policy may:

```text
add constraints
specialize behavior
```

but should not automatically be able to weaken higher-order requirements.

The exact override rules belong in specification.

---

# Deny versus obligation

Two policy outcomes should remain distinct.

```text
DENY
```

means:

> The operation may not proceed under the current conditions.

```text
OBLIGATION
```

means:

> The operation may proceed if additional required conditions are satisfied.

This distinction makes automated workflows much more useful.

---

# Conditional authorization

Authorization may depend on future obligations.

For example:

```text
Change approved for execution
provided that:

  compatibility suite passes
  security scan passes
  no unexpected impact appears
```

The resulting state may be:

```text
conditionally authorized
```

rather than fully complete.

This is especially useful for CI-driven workflows.

---

# Authorization expiry

Authorization should sometimes expire.

For example:

```text
approval granted
against revision abc123
```

may no longer be valid after:

```text
repository changed to def456
```

Likewise, temporary elevated authority may expire by time.

This helps prevent stale approval from becoming indefinite permission.

---

# Revision-aware governance

A governance decision may bind to:

```text
Plan revision
Target revision
Policy revision
Constitution version
```

If any materially relevant state changes, reevaluation may be required.

Conceptually:

```text
authorized against state A
        │
        ▼
state becomes B
        │
        ▼
authorization may be stale
```

This is essential for concurrent engineering systems.

---

# Policy versioning

Policies evolve.

A policy decision should therefore be capable of identifying:

```text
which policy version was evaluated
```

For example:

```text
Policy:
  POL-API-004@3
```

This allows later reconstruction of why an operation was permitted or denied.

---

# Governance events

Important governance events may include:

```text
authority.granted
authority.revoked
policy.changed
operation.denied
approval.granted
approval.revoked
exception.created
exception.expired
constitutional.amended
```

These may feed:

* observability,
* provenance,
* audit,
* reconciliation,
* and lifecycle behavior.

Not every low-level policy evaluation needs permanent storage.

Significant governance changes often do.

---

# Auditability

Monad should support reconstruction of significant governance decisions.

For an important operation, it should eventually be possible to answer:

```text
Who acted?

What were they allowed to do?

Which policy applied?

Which decision was made?

Who approved it?

Which evidence existed?

Which revision was evaluated?

What side effects occurred?
```

This is not only a compliance feature.

It is part of engineering explainability.

---

# Audit is not surveillance

The objective is not to preserve every human or machine action forever.

Monad should retain governance information when its reconstruction value justifies the cost.

The same principle applied elsewhere remains relevant:

> **Preserve information that is expensive or important to reconstruct.**

Governance metadata should remain purposeful.

---

# Governance failures

Governance itself can fail.

Potential failure modes include:

```text
ambiguous Actor identity
missing authority
conflicting policy
stale approval
expired delegation
invalid lifecycle transition
missing evidence
unknown scope
constitutional conflict
unavailable authoritative source
```

These should become explicit diagnostics.

---

# Fail closed versus fail open

Different operations require different behavior when governance information is unavailable.

For high-risk operations:

```text
cannot determine authorization
      ↓
deny / require escalation
```

may be appropriate.

For harmless read-only inspection:

```text
authority metadata unavailable
      ↓
return result with warning
```

may be more useful.

The policy system should allow risk-sensitive failure behavior.

---

# Governance uncertainty

Governance can itself contain uncertainty.

For example:

```text
Actor identity confirmed
Target identity ambiguous
Applicable policy uncertain
```

The system should not automatically interpret ambiguity as permission.

A governance decision may therefore return:

```text
undetermined
```

or:

```text
requires resolution
```

where appropriate.

---

# Governance and native systems

Native systems may already have their own authorization models.

Examples:

```text
filesystem permissions
GitHub permissions
cloud IAM
database roles
CI permissions
```

Monad should not pretend these do not exist.

Instead:

```text
Monad governance
      +
native authorization
```

both apply.

Monad may permit an operation that the native system ultimately refuses.

Or the native system may technically permit an operation that Monad policy prohibits.

---

# Native permission does not imply Monad authority

For example:

```text
GitHub token:
  can merge pull requests
```

does not establish:

```text
Monad policy:
  this Actor may merge this pull request
```

Monad governance operates above raw technical capability.

---

# Governance should not duplicate native enforcement unnecessarily

Where a native system already strongly enforces a rule, Monad may model and rely on that enforcement.

For example:

```text
cloud IAM
```

may remain the primary enforcement mechanism for cloud-resource access.

Monad can preserve:

* intended authority,
* policy,
* provenance,
* and relationships

without reimplementing the entire IAM system.

---

# Policy enforcement points

Policies may be enforced at different boundaries.

Examples include:

```text
planning
authorization
execution
lifecycle transition
release
projection publication
adapter invocation
```

A policy should be enforced where violation can still be prevented meaningfully.

---

# Defense in depth

Critical rules may be checked more than once.

For example:

```text
Planning:
  identifies public API change

Policy:
  requires compatibility review

Execution:
  verifies authorization still current

Release:
  verifies compatibility evidence exists
```

This is not necessarily redundant.

Each check protects a different transition.

---

# Governance of extensions

Extensions introduce new capabilities and therefore new risk.

An extension should declare:

```text
identity
version
capabilities
permissions
native integrations
schema contributions
```

Governance can then determine whether it may be activated.

An extension should not automatically gain all host-process authority.

---

# Extension capability boundary

For example:

```text
Extension:
  GitHub Project Adapter

Requires:
  read-project
  update-project-item

Does not require:
  delete-repository
  modify-organization-security
```

Capability declarations make least-authority extension models possible.

---

# Governance of AI providers

AI providers introduce privacy, data-handling, and capability concerns.

Policy may govern:

```text
which data may leave local environment
which provider may receive sensitive context
which model capability is permitted
whether tool use is allowed
whether output requires human review
```

Provider selection can therefore be a governance concern.

---

# Governance of context

Even reading information can require policy.

For example:

```text
Agent:
  allowed to modify package A

but:
  should not receive production credentials
  unrelated customer data
  private security material
```

The Context Component should respect governance when assembling task context.

This is an important form of least authority.

---

# Information disclosure policy

Policies may constrain:

```text
what an Actor can read
```

as well as:

```text
what an Actor can change
```

For example:

```text
public documentation agent
  may read public project artifacts

security analysis agent
  may read restricted security artifacts

implementation agent
  may receive only task-relevant secrets
```

Governance therefore applies to information flow as well as mutation.

---

# Governance of generated artifacts

Generated output may require review before promotion.

For example:

```text
AI-generated specification
      │
      ▼
draft
      │
      ▼
review
      │
      ▼
accepted specification
```

The generated artifact exists before it becomes authoritative.

Governance controls the promotion.

---

# Promotion of inferred information

The same principle applies to inference.

For example:

```text
inferred relationship
      │
      ▼
candidate
      │
      ▼
validation
      │
      ▼
confirmed relationship
```

Policy may allow automatic promotion for low-risk high-confidence inference.

More consequential identity or authority claims may require human confirmation.

---

# Governance and correction

Incorrect authoritative information must be correctable.

The governance system should support:

```text
correction proposal
      ↓
authority evaluation
      ↓
supporting evidence
      ↓
approval if required
      ↓
new authoritative state
```

while preserving the earlier record.

Correction should not require pretending the previous state never existed.

---

# Governance and rollback

Rollback may itself be governed.

A previous version having existed does not automatically mean any Actor may restore it.

For example:

```text
rollback production release
```

may require release authority.

Governance must apply to reversal as well as forward change.

---

# Governance and release

Release is one of the most consequential governance boundaries.

A release may require:

```text
required artifacts complete
specifications active
tests passed
security checks passed
migration ready
known risks accepted
release approval
```

The Release Component evaluates these requirements with the relevant policy and evidence.

---

# Release authority

Different Actors may have authority to:

```text
prepare release candidate
```

versus:

```text
approve release
```

versus:

```text
publish release
```

Those should not automatically be the same capability.

This enables separation of duties where needed.

---

# Governance and environment

Authority may vary by environment.

For example:

```text
Agent may deploy to:
  development

Agent may deploy to:
  staging after tests

Agent may not deploy to:
  production
```

Environment is therefore a possible policy scope.

---

# Governance and reversibility

Reversible operations may safely permit more autonomy than irreversible ones.

For example:

```text
generate local projection
  highly reversible
```

versus:

```text
delete production data
  potentially irreversible
```

Reversibility can therefore participate in risk evaluation.

---

# Governance and blast radius

A change affecting:

```text
one internal file
```

may require less governance than one affecting:

```text
public package API
100 downstream repositories
production infrastructure
```

Impact analysis can feed governance decisions.

Conceptually:

```text
Proposed Change
      │
      ▼
Impact Analysis
      │
      ▼
Risk Assessment
      │
      ▼
Policy Evaluation
```

This connects the graph directly to safer automation.

---

# Governance and unknown impact

Unknown impact should increase caution rather than disappear from evaluation.

For example:

```text
Known dependents:
  4

Unknown external dependents:
  possible
```

may trigger:

```text
human review required
```

even if all known automated checks pass.

---

# Governance and trust

Monad should not reduce trust to a single numerical score.

Trust may depend on:

* Actor identity,
* provenance,
* authority,
* evidence quality,
* source,
* verification,
* history,
* and scope.

Governance should use explicit facts where possible rather than vague universal trust rankings.

---

# Governance and history

Historical governance records remain useful.

For example:

```text
2026:
  Agent class A prohibited from release

2027:
  new verification system introduced

Policy changed:
  Agent class A may release low-risk packages
```

Both states matter historically.

A governance system should preserve which policy governed an action at the time it occurred.

---

# Current policy versus historical policy

This distinction mirrors the wider System model:

```text
Policy valid at time T
```

is not necessarily:

```text
Policy active now
```

Governance evaluation should use policy valid for the relevant operation and time.

---

# A governed change example

Consider an AI agent asked to change an authentication API.

The governance flow might look like:

```text
Request
   │
   ▼
Actor Resolution
   │
   ▼
Capability Check
   │
   ▼
Target Resolution
   │
   ▼
Impact Analysis
   │
   ▼
Applicable Policy Resolution
   │
   ▼
Risk Evaluation
   │
   ▼
Authority Evaluation
   │
   ▼
Decision:
  permit-with-obligations
   │
   ├── architecture review
   ├── compatibility verification
   └── specification update
   │
   ▼
Required Approval
   │
   ▼
Authorized Plan
   │
   ▼
Execution
   │
   ▼
Verification
   │
   ▼
Obligations Satisfied
   │
   ▼
Lifecycle Transition
```

Governance participates throughout the workflow.

It is not merely a permission check at the beginning.

---

# Example: low-risk autonomous change

Suppose an agent updates an internal test fixture.

Policy may determine:

```text
Actor:
  implementation-agent

Scope:
  current Work Package

Change:
  internal test fixture

Risk:
  low

Decision:
  permit

Obligations:
  affected test suite must pass

Approval:
  none required
```

The agent can proceed autonomously.

This is governed automation without unnecessary ceremony.

---

# Example: high-risk change

Now suppose the same agent proposes changing a public authentication protocol.

Policy may determine:

```text
Risk:
  high

Required:
  specification change
  architecture review
  compatibility analysis
  security verification
  human approval

Decision:
  cannot execute until obligations satisfied
```

The same Actor may still participate heavily.

Its autonomy envelope becomes narrower because the consequences are greater.

---

# Example: constitutional change

Suppose an Actor proposes changing the definition of authority itself.

That should cross a fundamentally different boundary.

```text
ordinary Actor authority
       │
       ✕ insufficient
       │
       ▼
constitutional amendment process required
```

The system should not let a policy modify the rules used to determine whether that policy is valid.

---

# Governance anti-patterns

Monad should actively guard against several failure modes.

## Capability equals permission

A tool can perform an operation, therefore it is allowed.

## Credential equals authority

Possession of a token is treated as governance.

## Role equals unlimited authority

A broad label such as "maintainer" silently grants every operation.

## Boolean policy only

Conditional obligations cannot be represented.

## Approval without scope

Approval becomes reusable indefinitely.

## Invisible exceptions

Engineers bypass policy without durable record.

## Policy scattered through code

Rules cannot be inspected or evaluated consistently.

## AI privilege

Agent-generated changes bypass ordinary review or evidence requirements.

## Human privilege by assumption

Human actions bypass rules merely because the Actor is human.

## Governance everywhere

Every trivial operation receives expensive approval ceremony.

## Constitution inflation

Ordinary project policy becomes nearly impossible to change because everything is treated as foundational.

---

# Governance design principles

A healthy governance model should follow several principles.

## Explicit over implicit

Important authority and restrictions should be representable.

## Scoped over global

Authority should be no broader than necessary.

## Revocable over permanent

Delegation should be removable or expire.

## Explainable over opaque

Actors should be able to understand policy outcomes.

## Evidence-aware

Important claims and transitions can require support.

## Risk-proportional

Governance should increase with consequence.

## Automation-friendly

Low-risk operations should be governable without constant manual intervention.

## Human-compatible

Governance should remain understandable to engineers rather than becoming an unreadable policy machine.

## Revision-aware

Approval should not silently outlive the state it evaluated.

## Historically inspectable

Past decisions should remain reconstructable.

---

# Governance as a control plane

One way to understand Monad governance is as a control plane over engineering capability.

```text
Engineering Capability
       │
       ▼
Governance
       │
       ├── who?
       ├── what?
       ├── where?
       ├── under which rules?
       ├── with which evidence?
       └── requiring whose approval?
       │
       ▼
Permitted Action
```

The control plane does not replace the engineering tools.

It determines how their capabilities may be exercised.

---

# Governance should remain inspectable

An engineer should increasingly be able to ask:

```text
Why can't I perform this operation?
```

and receive an answer such as:

```text
Operation:
  publish release

Actor:
  implementation-agent

Capability:
  publish-release available technically

Authority:
  not granted for production releases

Applicable policy:
  POL-REL-002

Required authority:
  release-authority

Required evidence:
  release verification bundle

Current result:
  denied
```

This is far more useful than:

```text
permission denied
```

---

# Governance queries

Monad should eventually support questions such as:

```text
What may this Actor do?

Who may modify this Subject?

Which policies apply here?

Why was this operation denied?

What approvals are required?

Which obligations remain unsatisfied?

Who granted this authority?

When does this authority expire?

Which policy version governed this operation?

Which Subjects are protected?

Which operations require human review?
```

Governance should be queryable like the rest of the system.

---

# Governance state

Governance itself becomes semantic state.

Examples include:

```text
authority assignments
delegations
policy versions
approval records
exception records
obligations
protected scopes
```

These states need:

* identity,
* lifecycle,
* provenance,
* authority,
* and history

like other meaningful Monad entities.

---

# Governance must govern itself

This creates an unavoidable recursive question:

> Who may change governance?

Monad addresses this through hierarchy.

Conceptually:

```text
ordinary policy
   governed by
      │
      ▼
higher-order governance
      │
      ▼
Constitution
```

The Constitution cannot eliminate recursion entirely.

It establishes the deliberately explicit root rules by which that recursion is managed.

---

# Governance versus organizational governance

This page describes governance **inside the Monad system**.

Examples:

```text
Can this Actor modify this Subject?

Which approval is required?

Which policy applies?

Can this release be published?
```

Organizational governance asks different questions:

```text
Who are the Monad maintainers?

How are project leaders chosen?

How are community decisions made?

Who owns the organization?

How are contributors recognized?
```

Those concerns belong outside this System section.

The two domains may interact, but they should not be conflated.

---

# Governance versus security

Security and governance overlap.

Security asks questions such as:

```text
Can an unauthorized Actor gain access?
Can credentials be stolen?
Can malicious input escape a boundary?
```

Governance asks:

```text
Even if the Actor technically has access,
is this operation legitimate?
```

Strong systems need both.

---

# Governance versus workflow

A workflow says:

```text
do A, then B, then C
```

Governance says:

```text
who may perform each step
under what conditions
and what makes the transition legitimate
```

Workflow can implement governance.

It should not be mistaken for governance itself.

---

# Governance versus verification

Verification asks:

```text
Did the implementation satisfy the contract?
```

Governance asks:

```text
Was the operation authorized and were
the required conditions satisfied?
```

A perfectly correct implementation can still be unauthorized.

An authorized operation can still fail verification.

Both dimensions matter.

```text
   │
   ▼
```

# Governance quality criteria

A strong governance system should be:

## Explicit

Important rules are inspectable.

## Scoped

Authority is limited to what is necessary.

## Composable

Multiple policies can apply without undefined behavior.

## Explainable

Decisions expose reasons and obligations.

## Enforceable

Rules affect actual execution boundaries.

## Risk-proportional

Low-risk work remains efficient.

## Revocable

Authority can expire or be removed.

## Revision-aware

Stale authorization is detectable.

## Historically traceable

Past decisions can be reconstructed.

## Extensible

New domains can define policy without rewriting the core.

## Secure

Actors cannot trivially bypass the governance boundary.

The full governance model does not need to appear in the first implementation.

A staged progression might be:

```text
Stage 1
  Actors
  Capabilities
  Scope

Stage 2
  Policy evaluation
  explicit permit / deny

Stage 3
  obligations
  approvals
```

The conceptual model can remain stable while implementation depth increases.

# Minimum viable governance

The smallest useful governance kernel likely requires:

```text
Actor identity
      +
Capability
      +
Scope
      +
Policy
      +
Policy Decision
      +
Execution enforcement
```

This enables the fundamental invariant:

> **An operation does not become legitimate merely because a tool is technically capable of performing it.**

More advanced governance can build from there.

# A governance decision model

At its simplest, the decision pipeline is:

```text
Who?
  Actor

Can?
  Capability

Where?
  Scope

What?
  Operation

Against what?
  Subject / Resource

Under which rules?
  Policy

With what authority?
  Authority

With what additional requirements?
  Obligations

With what support?
  Evidence / Approval

Result?
  Governance Decision
```

This provides a consistent way to reason about human, machine, automated, and extension behavior.

# Governance invariants

Several invariants should hold across the system.

## Capability does not imply authority

Technical possibility alone is insufficient.

## Authority is scoped

No Actor should implicitly gain universal authority.

## Authority has provenance

Important delegation should be explainable.

## Policy decisions are contextual

Actor, target, state, operation, and scope matter.

## Approval binds to scope and state

Approval should not become an unlimited reusable token.

## Obligations remain visible until resolved

Conditional authorization should not masquerade as completion.

## Constitutional rules cannot be modified through ordinary authority

System self-governance requires a stronger boundary.

## Human Actors are not automatically exempt

Governance can apply to humans and machines.

## AI Actors are not automatically privileged

Machine-generated work follows ordinary semantic and governance rules.

## Native permissions do not define Monad authority

Technical credentials remain distinct from legitimate permission.

## Exceptions are explicit

Exceptional action should remain inspectable.

## Governance history is preserved

Past policy state should remain reconstructable.

```text
Intent
  │
  ▼
Plan
  │
  ▼
Impact
  │
  ▼
Governance Evaluation
  │
  ▼
Authorized Action
  │
  ▼
Execution
  │
  ▼

  │
  ▼

  │
  ▼

  │
  ▼
```

```text
too much risk
```

or:

```text
too much friction
```

# The governing principle

Monad governance can be summarized as:

> **Engineering capability should be exercised only within explicit, inspectable authority whose scope, conditions, obligations, and consequences are understood well enough to govern the change safely.**

The goal is not to stop humans or machines from acting.

The goal is to make increasingly powerful action **bounded, explainable, and trustworthy**.
